Ishval was not the place Ed remembered.

More precisely, it was changing.

After he got off the train and traveled for a good while, the town he arrived at was lined with newly built houses. Some still smelled of fresh brick. Others didn’t even have roofs yet. On one side of the road, workers were burying water pipes. A little further on, there was a small schoolhouse, with children running around it.

Ed slowed down.

The Ishval he’d seen before hadn’t been like this. Collapsed buildings. Abandoned towns. Desert and ruins. And the people who’d survived.

The traces of the war hadn’t disappeared now, either. Between the new buildings, blackened walls still stood. So did toppled pillars. Some of them looked like they’d been left that way on purpose.

“Edward Elric.”

He turned at the voice.

“Miles.”

Miles walked over. He hadn’t changed much. Only the insignia on his collar was different. The man who had been a Major was a Colonel now.

Ed raised a hand. “Been a while.”

“I heard you were out in the far west. You got here fast.”

“I didn’t sleep on the train.”

Miles looked Ed up and down. “Your height does not seem to have changed much.”

“That’s the first thing you’re gonna say?!”

A soldier working nearby flinched and turned. Miles started walking as if nothing had happened.

“Follow me.”

“Take that back!”

“How do I take back a fact?”

“You’re starting to sound just like that Colonel!”

“He’s the Fuhrer now.”

“I know, I know!”

Ed grumbled and followed him.

The closer they got to the center of town, the more people there were. Ishvalans were hauling materials. Soldiers in Amestrian uniforms moved alongside them. Stacking bricks. Carrying timber. Clearing the roads.

On the surface, it all looked fine. But Ed noticed something odd.

They were using the same road, and yet they didn’t mix.

When a few Ishvalans passed, the soldiers stepped slightly aside. When soldiers carried materials, the Ishvalans drifted naturally the other way. Nobody had given an order. Nobody was fighting.

There was just a bit of distance.

Ed asked, “Is it always like that?”

Miles looked away. “It is much better than it was.”

“Yeah?”

“At first, working in the same place was hard enough.”

Miles spoke facing forward.

“You build houses. You build roads. You lay water lines.”

“Yeah.”

“Does that mean Ishval is coming back?”

Ed didn’t answer.

“The question,” Miles said, “is what we are rebuilding.”

He stopped there. He didn’t explain further, and Ed didn’t ask.

A little later, Ed spotted a familiar back. The large frame. Dark skin. The scar across his forehead. The corner of Ed’s mouth lifted.

“Hey!”

The man turned. Red eyes settled on Ed. A moment of silence.

Then—

“Fullmetal.”

Ed’s face crumpled at once. “You too?!”

Scar narrowed his eyes. “What do you mean?”

“I’m not a State Alchemist anymore. I can’t even use alchemy.”

Scar considered this. “Edward.”

“…”

“What.”

“…Just call me Fullmetal.”

“You are particular.”

He could hear Miles trying to stifle a laugh.

“Don’t laugh!”

Scar was helping with the reconstruction, hauling timber and clearing collapsed walls.

Ed watched him for a moment. It would have been hard to imagine once. A man who had gone around killing State Alchemists was now putting up a wall.

Scar asked, “Why did you come?”

“I’ve got something to ask.”

“Me?”

“About your brother, actually.”

Scar’s hand paused for an instant. Ed didn’t miss it.

“Al went to Xing.”

“Alphonse did?”

“To learn alkahestry.”

Scar nodded. “That sounds like him.”

“He sent a letter from there.” Ed folded his arms. “He said he wants to know how your brother learned alkahestry.”

Scar didn’t answer.

“Now that I think about it, it’s weird.”

“What is?”

“Your brother was an Ishvalan.”

“Yes.”

“Alchemy itself was practically forbidden here.”

“Yes.”

“And he didn’t only study Amestrian alchemy. He knew Xingese alkahestry too.”

Ed stepped closer. “Where did he learn it?”

Scar studied him for a moment. “I only know that he heard about it from someone in a Xingese caravan.”

“Who?”

“I do not know.”

“…What?”

“I did not know everything my brother was researching.”

“You didn’t hear anything at all?”

“No.”

“Records?”

Scar’s eyes moved, just slightly. Ed caught it immediately.

“You’ve got something.”

“…”

“You do, don’t you?”

“Some.”

Ed’s eyes lit up. “Show me.”

“No.”

“Why?!”

“I have work to do now.”

“After, then.”

“I will think about it.”

“That means no.”

Scar picked up the timber again. Ed followed behind him.

“Just once.”

“No.”

“I’ll be careful.”

“No.”

“I’ll wash my hands.”

“That is not the problem.”

“Then what is?”

Scar stopped and looked at him. “You are annoying.”

“That’s your reason?!”

In the end, Ed simply trailed after Scar as he hauled timber. Miles, unable to watch any longer, grabbed Ed by the back of his collar.

“Let go!”

“Stop bothering a man who is working.”

“I just want that notebook—”

“The notebook comes later.”

“You have heard about the underground structure from the Fuhrer, I assume.”

Ed went quiet. “…That.”

“The locals have checked the inside. They think it may be an old prayer hall.”

“Can I go in?”

“Some areas.”

Miles said, “On the condition that you touch nothing.”

“What do you take me for?”

Miles and Scar both looked at Ed at the same time.

“Why are you both looking at me?!”

Miles turned to Scar. “I need an Ishvalan’s eyes there. Would you come and look?”

“…Very well.”

“Then the notebook—”

“After we get back.”

Scar set down the timber.

The stairs leading underground were new. When the ground had partly caved in during the reconstruction, they said, the entrance had been exposed.

Below, it was different. Stone walls. A low ceiling. Old air. Instead of torches, temporary lamps had been set up.

Two elderly Ishvalans were already waiting inside. Ed lowered his voice without thinking.

Miles said, “We believe it is a place of prayer.”

“How old is it?”

“We do not know exactly.”

“Before the civil war?”

One of the old men answered. “Long before that.”

Ed examined the walls. Letters. Symbols. Human figures. Lines spreading like rays of sun. And the floor.

He stopped.

There was a huge circular pattern. Within the circle, a number of lines crossed. Along the lines, small figures repeated.

Ed crouched. “…That’s a transmutation circle.”

Scar spoke behind him. “It is not.”

Ed looked back. “Then what is it?”

“A prayer.”

“This?”

“Yes.”

Ed looked at the floor again. No matter how he looked, it resembled one. The circle. The lines. The repeating geometric structure. Anyone who had drawn transmutation circles thousands of times would see it that way.

But looking closer, something was off. There were no symbols for the composition of matter or the transmutation process, as in an ordinary circle. The start and end of the lines weren’t clear either.

If anything…

They connected.

“Did people actually use this?”

“It is said people stood around it when they prayed.”

“What about alchemy?”

The old man shook his head. “There is no such story.”

Ed rested his chin on his hand. “Then why does it look like this…?”

As his eyes followed the lines, he noticed something small. At each crossing, a stone had been set into the floor. Dark red. About the size of a fingertip joint.

Ed brought his face closer. “What are these?”

The old man said, “We sometimes see this stone in old places of prayer.”

“What’s it called?”

The old man said a short word in the Ishvalan language. Ed tried to repeat it and gave up.

“What does it mean?”

“It is difficult to translate.”

Ed studied the stone. “Has anyone ever pulled one out?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

The old man looked at him as if he’d asked something strange. “Because it is not something one pulls out.”

Ed closed his mouth.

Scar said beside him, “Do not touch it.”

“I didn’t.”

“Your hand was moving.”

Ed quietly withdrew it. “I was just observing.”

“With your hand?”

“Touch counts as observation.”

“Do not.”

“Fine.”

Ed looked over the whole pattern again. Prayer. A structure that looked like a transmutation circle. The stones. And Scar’s brother’s research.

Something seemed about to connect in his head, then slipped away. It wasn’t enough yet.

Just before sunset, Ed latched onto Scar again.

“You’re done working now, right?”

“It seems so.”

“Then show me.”

“What.”

“Your brother’s records.”

“I said I would think about it.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“No.”

“Hey!”

Scar kept walking. Ed followed.

“Just once.”

“No.”

“I’ll only read it.”

“No.”

“I won’t take it.”

“Of course you will not.”

“Then what’s the problem?!”

“Fullmetal.”

“What.”

“You are loud.”

“So show me and I’ll be quiet.”

Scar stopped. Ed stopped too. They looked at each other for a moment.

Scar sighed. “Follow me.”

Ed grinned. “Should’ve done that sooner.”

Scar’s house was plainer than Ed had expected. One small room. A low table. Bedding. A few changes of clothes. That was all.

Ed looked around as he came in. “You live here?”

“Yes.”

“I figured it’d be somewhere scarier.”

“What does that mean?”

“Nothing.”

Scar sat down against one wall and took a small box from the floor. He opened the lid. Inside were a few old notebooks and a bundle of papers.

The playfulness left Ed’s face.

Scar took the topmost notebook and set it on the table. Tap.

“Handle it carefully.”

Ed stared at it. “If you had something like this, you should’ve said so from the start!”

“I did not know what it was,” Scar said. “I kept it because it was my brother’s. I opened it once or twice and left it as it was.”

“You sat on this for years…?” Ed glared at the notebook. “Then why’d you play hard to get?”

“I changed my mind.”

“Did my persistence move you?”

“You would have followed me around more.”

“That’s called persistence.”

Ed opened the notebook.

The first page was alchemy. Equivalent Exchange. A transmutation circle. A few clumsy diagrams. A few pages later, the character of the handwriting changed. It was diary-like notes, jotted down day by day, more words than sentences.

Little brother got into trouble again today. The neighbor’s fence this time.

Ed looked up. “Sounds like you.”

“Do not read that.”

“Were you a troublemaker as a kid?”

“Do not read that.”

Ed turned the page, holding back a laugh.

Prayer. Memorized since childhood.

Don’t know what it means.

Brother ate my bread and swore he didn’t. Big fight.

Repairing the well in the rain. My back.

The well’s dropped again. Measured the channel width and the slope of the ground again.

The market ink is watery. Different shop next time.

The next page.

Ishvala = the Truth?

“…Truth?”

Ed read it under his breath. Scar looked up. “What is it?”

“Nothing.”

Calling a god “truth” was something every religion did. Ed turned the page without much thought.

Brother’s shoes ripped again.

The next page.

Human transmutation — taboo.

The Gate appears.

Ed’s hand stopped. His expression hardened. His eyes raced over the words. One line. Two. Then back to the beginning.

“…Wait.”

Scar looked at him. “What is wrong?”

No answer.

This man had known. Ed couldn’t tell how far his knowledge went. But he’d at least known what those words meant.

In that instant, Ed’s eyes moved.

“…The Truth.”

“What?”

Ed began flipping back through the earlier pages. The line he’d passed over so casually before showed up again.

Ishvala = the Truth?

His hand stopped. He went back to the page with human transmutation, then forward again.

Human transmutation.

The Truth.

The two phrases fused in his head. The carelessness that had been in his face a moment ago was gone.

“…No way.”

Scar narrowed his eyes. “What has gotten into you?”

Ed didn’t answer. He closed the notebook.

“I need to think.”

He tucked it under his arm and stood.

“That is my brother’s.”

“I know.”

“Then put it down.”

“I’m just borrowing it today.”

“I did not give permission.”

“I’ll give it back tomorrow!”

Ed was almost to the door when Scar spoke again.

“Fullmetal. What did you see?”

Ed started to answer, then stopped. Not yet. Something had come to mind, but there were still too many blanks to say it out loud.

“I don’t know yet.”

“…”

“But there’s something there.”

Ed glanced down at the notebook under his arm. “Let me think a little more. I’ll come back tomorrow.”

“Fullmetal.”

“I really will give it back!”

“That is not what I meant.”

Ed turned around. Scar still looked baffled. But Ed was already looking somewhere else.

Human transmutation. The Truth. And the question mark between them.

“We’ll talk tomorrow.”

The door closed.

Scar didn’t chase him. He stared at the closed door for a long while.

That night, under the lamp in his temporary lodging, Ed read the rest of the notebook to the end.

A person from Xing. Something called alkahestry.

Similar to prayer?

Did alkahestry come from prayer, or prayer from alkahestry?

In the margin beside a transcribed prayer, he found an unfamiliar word.

Karsa. East. The end.

He couldn’t tell whether it was the name of a person or a place.

Ed took out his notebook, picked up a pen, and thought for a moment. Then he wrote one line.

What is Ishvala?

He looked at the sentence.

When he’d come to Ishval, there had been one thing he wanted to know: how had Scar’s brother learned alkahestry? And yet, one day after arriving, he had one more question.

The corner of his mouth lifted. “Now it’s getting fun.”

There was only one line on the page.

What is Ishvala?

Back then, Ed still believed the answer was already half in sight.
