The innkeeper held out a thick envelope.

“This came for you, Mr. Elric. Been through three cities to find you, by the look of it.”

Ed checked the back first.

Alphonse Elric.

A Xing postmark, with a pile of Amestrian forwarding stamps overlapping it.

The letter was longer than he’d expected. He sat on the edge of the bed, read the first page, and turned to the second. By the third, he was muttering.

“…Is this guy traveling or studying?”

Outside the window, a train rumbled past.

It had been nearly two years since Al had gone east and Ed had gone west. On the platform that day, Al had been the first to board the eastbound train. Leaning on the window frame with his recovered body, he’d smiled.

“You’re going west first, right, Brother?”

“Yeah.”

“I’ll write.”

“Make sure you eat.”

“That’s supposed to be my line for you.”

The train had pulled out, and Ed had walked to the platform on the other side.

He hadn’t been surprised when Al said he wanted to learn alkahestry firsthand. The two of them had always looked at the same thing and dug into it from opposite directions. This time, Al had just picked his direction first.

Dear Brother,

I finally made it to the capital of Xing.

It’s a lot bigger than I expected. It feels completely different from Central.

I met May, too. She was so happy that I was coming, and then on my first day she gave me a three-hour lecture on alkahestry.

Ed snorted.

And I met Ling.

Ed’s face scrunched up immediately.

I thought he might’ve grown up a little now that he’s emperor, but he hasn’t changed much.

He stole one of my buns.

“The guy’s an emperor and he still steals other people’s food?”

The next line.

He said it was a tax.

“That’s just theft!”

Something thumped against the wall of the next room.

Ed shut his mouth.

After a moment, he went back to the letter.

The first two pages were mostly about life in Xing. The food. The streets. May. The people who taught alkahestry. A story about getting lost in the imperial palace.

Then, on the third page, the handwriting got a little more cramped.

Alkahestry is a lot more different than you made it sound.

It isn’t just the shape of the circles.

In Amestris, we explain it as drawing power from the earth.

But here, they think the earth’s power is already flowing, and that you read that flow and make use of it.

Ed sat up straighter.

This was a little different from what he knew. Of course he knew alkahestry existed. He’d watched May use it. He’d known it worked differently from Amestrian alchemy. He’d even heard the old tale that the Sage of the West had brought knowledge to Xing, and that it had mingled with the local medicine and shaped how alkahestry developed.

But that was a story about origins.

How they actually understood the world was another matter entirely.

He read the next line.

People here call it the Dragon’s Pulse.

They believe there’s a flow of power not just in mountains and rivers but through the whole earth, and that people live inside it too.

May says the important thing in alkahestry isn’t creating power. It’s reading the flow.

Ed set the letter down.

“The flow…”

It wasn’t an unfamiliar idea. Alchemy considered the movement of energy as well. Tectonic shifts. Heat. The composition of matter. Deconstruction and reconstruction.

But this wasn’t a matter of wording. The starting point was different.

An Amestrian alchemist asked first: What will I make, and from what? What do I need? How do I deconstruct it, and how do I reconstruct it?

Al’s letter made alkahestry sound like it started somewhere else entirely. It sounded like it started by looking at where something that already existed was flowing, and where it was going.

Ed picked the letter up again.

I might not have understood it properly yet.

“Yeah, you probably haven’t.”

But I think you’d have loved seeing it in person.

“Obviously.”

You’d have probably asked questions all day, though.

“That’s called studying.”

Ed’s mouth stopped on the next line.

There’s one strange thing, though.

It’s been on my mind the whole way to Xing.

Without realizing it, he pulled the page closer.

Scar’s brother.

For a while, he only stared at the words.

Scar’s brother.

The two arms Scar had carried through the Ishvalan Civil War. Deconstruction in the right. Reconstruction in the left. Built from research that combined Amestrian alchemy and Xingese alkahestry.

Back then there had been too much else going on. The Philosopher’s Stone. The homunculi. The national transmutation circle. Father. With the world about to be turned upside down, there’d been no room to calmly wonder how a dead scholar had reached that kind of knowledge.

Now it was different.

Al’s letter went on.

Brother.

How did Scar’s brother know about alkahestry?

Ed narrowed his eyes.

I never heard that he studied in Xing.

But if you look at the research he left behind, it doesn’t seem like he just copied a few circles.

That was true.

His research hadn’t been two systems forced together. At the very least, there was evidence he’d tried to understand the difference between them.

Ed stood up from the bed.

He’d known the story that alkahestry was influenced by the Sage of the West. But that was a tale from hundreds of years ago. The Sage wasn’t what he was curious about right now.

Scar’s brother was.

Even he and Al had only started to grasp how alkahestry actually thought after meeting people in Xing. So how had a scholar from Ishval gotten that far? Where had he started?

Ed read the last page.

I asked May, but she doesn’t know Scar’s brother’s name.

I haven’t found any record of him on the alkahestry side yet, either.

I’ll keep looking here a little longer.

If you know anything, let me know.

And at the very end, it was the usual Al.

And eat properly.

Ed’s eyebrow twitched.

“I’m the older brother here.”

He folded the letter. Then unfolded it again.

How did Scar’s brother know about alkahestry?

He read only that line, once more.

The next day, Ed was in a small library.

It wasn’t big. This was a town he’d decided to stay in for a few days on the road, and it didn’t have much in the way of specialist alchemy materials. Still, there were things worth checking.

Ishval.

Alkahestry.

Xing.

Eastern medicine.

The history of alchemical exchange.

Two hours later.

“Nothing.”

The books stacked up on the desk.

Three hours later.

“Seriously, nothing.”

A library attendant glanced over. Ed lowered his voice.

“I mean, how can there be this little?”

Most of what he found on Ishval was about religion and the civil war. Alchemy barely came up. That made sense, in its way. In Ishval, alchemy itself had been close to taboo.

There were a few books on Xing’s alkahestry, but most were shallow accounts by travelers. A healing art, brilliant in the East. Transmutation possible from a distance. It draws on the energy of the earth.

That was all.

Nothing he wanted. Where Scar’s brother had started. What he’d read. Why an Ishvalan scholar had studied an Eastern art.

Nothing.

Ed shut the book.

“Guess I’ll just have to ask someone directly.”

The problem was who.

You couldn’t ask a dead man.

But his younger brother was still alive.

Ed rested his chin on his hand. “Where’s Scar these days?”

The answer came faster than he’d expected.

That evening, the inn’s attendant called to him.

“Mr. Elric?”

“Yeah?”

“There’s a call for you from Central.”

“For me?”

Ed took the receiver.

“Hello.”

A familiar voice answered.

“It’s been a while, Fullmetal.”

Click.

Ed hung up.

The attendant blinked.

A few seconds later, the phone rang again.

Ed picked up. “What.”

“Isn’t it only polite to answer when someone greets you?”

“I’m not Fullmetal anymore.”

“Old habits die hard.”

“I’m hanging up.”

“Wait.”

Ed’s hand stopped. The voice had turned a little serious.

“Where are you now?”

“Near the western border.”

“You’ve gone quite a distance.”

“So?”

“Would you be willing to go to Ishval?”

Ed’s expression changed. “Why?”

“During the reconstruction work, they found an old underground structure.”

“A structure?”

“We don’t know exactly what it is yet. According to Miles’s report, it’s likely an old place of prayer.”

Ed leaned against the wall. “When was it built?”

“Unknown.”

“What’s inside?”

“We’re checking.”

“Any writing?”

“Some, apparently.”

“Pictures? Diagrams?”

A short silence came over the line.

“…I knew you’d ask.”

“So what’s in there?”

“They say there are geometric patterns on the floor.”

Ed’s eyes narrowed. “A transmutation circle?”

“We don’t know that yet.”

“Photos?”

“Fullmetal.”

“Again, I’m not Fullmetal.”

“That isn’t the point.”

Mustang’s voice dropped.

“I didn’t call to ask you to investigate.”

Ed stopped.

“The site is very likely an Ishvalan religious structure.”

“…”

“Just because the military found it doesn’t mean the military has the right to go rummaging through it.”

Ed said nothing.

What would the old Mustang have done? Probably gotten whatever information he needed first. Of course, the Mustang of now couldn’t help being curious, either.

And yet he was waiting.

“Miles and the locals are examining it first. The religious leaders will take part as well.”

“So why are you telling me?”

“Because I received a report that you’ve been causing a commotion somewhere in the west.”

“I’m a civilian. Who’s reporting my location?”

“News that a former State Alchemist got into a fight with a station attendant travels faster than you’d think.”

“That’s because he started it. He made a crack about my—”

Ed stopped.

“Anyway,” Mustang said. “Scar happens to be in Ishval right now.”

Ed shifted the receiver. “Scar is?”

“He’s helping with the reconstruction, I hear.”

Ed remembered Al’s letter.

How did Scar’s brother know about alkahestry?

The corner of his mouth lifted.

“Perfect.”

“What is?”

“I happen to have something to ask him.”

“Fullmetal, I’ll say it again. The ruins—”

“Yeah, yeah. I won’t touch anything.”

“I’m not sure how much to trust your ‘I won’t touch anything.’”

“Then why’d you tell me?”

This time, Mustang was quiet for a moment.

“…That, it seems, was my mistake.”

“Too late.”

Ed hung up.

The next morning, a train bound east left the station on the western border.

Ed sat by the window and unfolded Al’s letter again.

The plains of a foreign country slid past outside. After a few days of travel, he’d cross the border and see Amestris’s plains. At its eastern end lay Ishval. And much farther east, beyond the desert, lay Xing.

He had looked at that direction on a map more than once. But he had never tied the whole stretch together with a single question.

Xing had a tale of the Sage of the West.

Ishval had a scholar who’d forbidden alchemy yet studied alkahestry.

And that scholar was Scar’s brother.

It was too early to say anything was connected. There was nothing yet. Only one more thing he didn’t know.

The old Ed would have hated not knowing. He still didn’t like it much. But strangely…

It was a little fun.

Ed folded the letter.

This time he wasn’t being asked to save the world. He didn’t have to get anyone back. There was just one thing he didn’t understand.

And he wasn’t the type to leave something like that alone.

He looked out the window.

“Well, I’ll find out when I get there.”

The train kept running east.

Xing was still far away.

First, there was Ishval.
