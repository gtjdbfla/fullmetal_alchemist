Ishval, Amestris.

By the time morning came, Ed still hadn’t closed his notebook. At the top of a new page was the sentence he had written last night.

What I learned this time.

Below it, it was still blank.

Ed tapped the page with the tip of his pen. “…What do I write first?”

When he had first come to Ishval, it had been much simpler. The records Scar’s brother left behind. The place of prayer. The stone. Ishvala. And the Truth. He had thought that once he found one answer, the next would follow.

Ed turned back through the notebook.

Ishvala = the Truth?

He looked at it for a long time. When he first wrote that sentence, he had been pretty confident. The old records had phrases that treated the world and people as one. They had resembled what he had seen at the Gate. So he had thought they might be the same thing.

Ed picked up his pen. He drew a line through the sentence.

Ishvala = the Truth?

He didn’t erase it completely. Beside it he wrote:

No basis. Being similar doesn’t make them the same.

“One.”

He turned the page.

The stone.

Ed snorted. “This one was just plain wrong.”

The dark red mineral wasn’t some secret of alchemy. It had nothing to do with the Philosopher’s Stone. There wasn’t any of it in Karsa at all. He was the one who had tied it to alchemy, just because it had been found in a place of prayer.

Ed wrote in large letters.

The stone = a mineral.

He thought for a moment and added one more line.

Meaning isn’t in the stone. People give it meaning.

To the people of old Ishval, a sacred stone. In Amestris, a material. In Xing, a gem. And to the people of Ishval now, a stone whose meaning they hadn’t decided yet.

Ed turned to the next page.

The Sage of the West.

Below it was writing from long ago.

Hohenheim?

And a name written over it later, in darker ink.

Van Hohenheim.

Ed’s hand stopped.

At first, Ed had thought of Hohenheim as the starting point of everything. But Karsa had had its own ways before Hohenheim ever came. The old An had practical knowledge too, for reading the paths of water and wind and the changes in the land. Xing had medicine. It had qi. It had a way of thinking that read the Dragon’s Pulse.

Hohenheim wasn’t the one who made those.

“He was learning,” Ed muttered.

There would have been things he brought from the west. There would have been things he picked up in the east. What exactly changed after those meetings, and how, couldn’t all be known. But he did know one thing.

Ed wrote:

Hohenheim wasn’t someone who brought the answers.

And below that:

He was looking for answers too.

He looked at that sentence for a long time.

The Hohenheim Ed remembered had always known too much. He’d lived longer than Ed. Seen more places than Ed. He’d always seemed like someone far ahead who already knew the answers. But the Hohenheim of four hundred years ago—had been asking about things he didn’t know.

“…I kind of like that.”

Next was Karsa.

The price?

The earth.

It’s all already here.

The water was already flowing, and the wind was already blowing. Karsa didn’t make what wasn’t there. They read what was there. They made roads. And let it flow along those roads.

Ed found the sentence he had written back then.

Where’s the boundary?

“Right.”

The problem had been less the law than how far he had been looking. Cut off just the result in front of you, look for the price paid right there, and the calculation didn’t add up. Look a little wider, and something had been moving from the start.

Ed wrote a line.

Karsa doesn’t break Equivalent Exchange.

And soon he frowned. “…No.”

He drew a line through it. The people of Karsa had never explained the world in terms of Equivalent Exchange to begin with. There was no need to force his words onto them.

He wrote again.

There was no basis for saying it contradicts the law I call Equivalent Exchange.

“That’s better.”

Next.

Where did alkahestry begin?

With this one too, he had tried to find a starting point at first. But he couldn’t pick just one. Xing had always had medicine, qi, and a way of thinking that looked at the Dragon’s Pulse, and people and knowledge from the west had come and gone on top of that. Knowledge that crossed over didn’t stay the way it was.

Ed read a sentence he had written down before.

Not who made it first.

What changed when they met.

This time he didn’t fix it. “This one was right.”

Below it was Kairun’s name. Ed’s expression hardened a little.

The man who had tried to kill the emperor. But the way he had worked was still vivid in Ed’s mind. One transmutation circle. And yet the transmutation didn’t stop at the floor. Floor to pillar. Pillar to wall. Wall to ceiling. Back to the floor.

Karsa made roads. Alkahestry read the roads already in the earth. Kairun—

“He didn’t look only at the earth.”

Wind. Pressure. Vibration. The connections in a structure. Roads that already existed. He carried them on with a single transmutation.

That didn’t mean Kairun had invented it. At least, there was no basis for saying so. Who he learned it from was unknown. Whether there had been others who read nature the same way was unknown too.

Ed wrote:

Kairun = founder?

Beside it, in large letters:

Unknown.

And below that:

What’s certain: the alkahestry I knew wasn’t the whole of it.

Ed grinned. “That works too.”

The hand turning the pages stopped.

Why do only Amestrian alchemists see the Gate?

No one had answered this one yet. The people of Karsa didn’t talk about a Gate. He’d never heard anything like that from May either. Kairun hadn’t either.

Without thinking, Ed gripped his right arm. Where the automail had once been attached.

Karsa didn’t put themselves at the center when they calculated nature. They found paths within what was already moving.

Amestrian alchemy always said the same thing. Comprehension, deconstruction, reconstruction. To gain something, you give something up. The more you try to gain, the more you give up, and at the end of that, a person stakes himself.

“…Is that why we see the Gate?”

No one answered.

After a long while, Ed wrote in the notebook.

Hypothesis.

Below it:

Amestrian alchemy puts the alchemist himself at the center of the exchange?

A structure where the bigger the transmutation, the more you end up putting yourself up as the price?

Is the Gate at the end of that?

Ed stopped his pen. In the old days, he would have connected the lines here. He would have thought he was right.

This time, at the end, he wrote in large letters:

Not confirmed.

“Right.” He pushed the notebook back. “If I think I’m right again, I’m in trouble.”

Around noon, Scar came by.

“Were you up all night?”

“A little.”

“Your face looks dead.”

“It’s naturally handsome.”

Scar didn’t respond.

Ed closed the notebook. “About your brother.”

Scar’s eyes moved.

“I think I sort of get now why he went east from Karsa.”

“Tell me.”

“I don’t think he went to bring back one answer.”

Learning in Karsa. Following the caravans into Lansi. Going deeper into Xing to find out more about alkahestry. And then coming back to Ishval. Rather than how to make water, how not to lose the water you have. What Ishval needed wasn’t one miraculous new technique. It was having more ways to choose from.

“Your brother came back,” Ed said. “With what he’d learned.”

“He brought back eyes that could see the problems of this land.”

Ed paused for a moment. “Yeah. Your brother saved this country—no, the whole world.”

Scar said nothing.

“And he left this behind.” Ed pointed to the old records beside his notebook.

Those records had sent him to Karsa. Made him find traces of Hohenheim in Karsa. And those traces had led to Xing.

“I don’t know if I found all the answers,” Ed said. “But it’s not over, right?”

“He is dead,” Scar said shortly.

“I know,” Ed answered right away. And after a moment, he added, “That’s why the people who are left keep looking.”

Scar looked at Ed.

“What your brother couldn’t find, I look for,” Ed said. “If I can’t find it, Al looks.” He thought for a moment. “And if that doesn’t work, somebody else will look.”

Scar was quiet for a while. “That does not sound like you.”

“What doesn’t?!”

“Before, you would have said you would figure it out yourself, all the way to the end.”

Ed closed his mouth. “…I guess so.”

Scar turned to go.

“You’re just leaving?” Ed asked.

“That was well thought.”

“Is that how you give a compliment?”

“I have never complimented you.”

“Why, you—!”

Afternoon.

Ed went down to the place of prayer where he had first found the stone. The entrance had been widened a little for the restoration work. On the inner wall, the old patterns remained as they were.

At first he had tried to find a single answer here. Now he only knew that there wasn’t a single starting point. He still didn’t know what Ishvala was. But he knew more than he had at the start.

Ed stood in front of the wall. It wasn’t a sentence copied down by hand. The words carved into the stone were still there.

The one who stood before the door saw the world.

And the world saw him.

At first he had read it as the Gate he himself had seen. What he had called the world. What he called the Truth.

This time he tried reading it differently. A person standing before the flow. The flow passes through him, and he knows that he is a part of it.

One who knows oneself knows a part of the world.

The flow may be borrowed. But one must never think of it as one’s own.

It resembled the way the people of Karsa read their roads. The lines had been there from the start.

Which reading was right, there was no way to know. The problem with old records was that they could be read both ways.

Amestrian alchemy puts the self at the center of the exchange. This record puts the self as one part of the flow.

Ed looked at that difference for a long time.

He thought of the line drawn on Kairun’s ankle. A transmutation circle drawn on the body in advance. The research of Scar’s brother, carved into Scar’s arm, was the same. The lines Majid said he had drawn the first few of. His brother had been someone who tested that possibility, and Kairun had been someone far better at reading that flow.

Lines carved into the body. If the body was part of nature too, maybe that was the closest place to touch the flow.

This was a hypothesis too.

But the thing he had been most curious about on this journey from the very beginning was alchemy outside Amestris. A piece of that answer was here.

He still didn’t know what Ishvala was.

Ed opened his notebook.

What I learned this time.

Below it, he wrote one by one.

Being similar doesn’t mean having the same origin.

When people meet, knowledge changes.

You can make a wrong story out of nothing but facts.

You have to think about where the information you find ends up going.

A good decision and a good way of deciding aren’t the same.

You can’t make decisions while leaving out the people affected.

The last line was empty. Ed thought for a long time. Then he wrote:

Having seen the Truth doesn’t mean knowing all of it.

This time he didn’t cross it out.

At the back of the notebook, a few questions still remained.

What is Ishvala?

Why do only Amestrian alchemists see the Gate?

Below that, a sentence he had written today.

Are Amestrian Equivalent Exchange and the Gate connected? — hypothesis.

And one more.

Who did Kairun learn from?

Ed rolled the tip of his pen, then wrote the last question.

What did Hohenheim see at the Gate?

Four. The questions left at the end of a journey he had started to find answers.

In the old days, they would have annoyed him. He was still curious. Curious enough to go crazy. But he no longer felt that not answering all of them right away meant failure.

When he closed the notebook and came outside, the sun was going down. People were coming and going between the houses being rebuilt. He could see people who had been fighting in yesterday’s meeting too. What to do about the dark red mineral still hadn’t been decided. But no one was secretly digging it up either.

That was enough. For now.

“Someone came from the east,” Miles said that evening.

Ed turned. “Who?”

“Rather than a person, documents came first.”

“What documents?”

Miles held out a sheet of paper. It was a translation of an official document from Xing.

Ed read it quickly. Southern trade road. His hand stopped at those words. “This…”

“It seems they’ve requested talks with the Ishval side too.”

The road wasn’t open yet. And it wasn’t the road that used to lead to Karsa. That road had been left off the map. It was the road Karsa had decided not to open again. Instead, a new route that went the long way around, farther south, was under consideration.

Land in the An region. Wells. Pasture. Right of passage. The effect on Lansi and Bai trade. And at the far western end—the possibility of connecting with Ishval.

Ed looked at the document for a long time. “Did the Emperor decide this?”

“It seems not.” Miles pointed lower down.

The imperial court of Xing. An representatives. Bai representatives. Lansi merchants. Representatives of the regions along the route. It said agreement was needed from each of them. And the Ishval section would not be settled without the consent of the Ishvalan side.

Ed laughed. “He really did it.”

“Did what?”

“That guy.”

Ling’s face came to mind. Instead of the emperor giving an order to open a road—an order that kept the emperor from deciding alone.

“Never mind.”

“What do you think?” Miles asked.

Ed didn’t answer right away. Someone had erased the old road. Karsa had decided not to open it again. So that road would not open. The new road was longer. It needed more wells. It cost more money. There was still a lot to negotiate.

Still—

“It’s good,” Ed said. “Even if it goes the long way around.”

Miles smiled. “Are you talking about the road?”

“Both.”

A few days later, the first survey party left Ishval.

They weren’t a caravan. Trade hadn’t started yet. People to check the route. People to survey the water. People to negotiate the terms of passage. And residents’ representatives taking part from Ishval.

A few wagons slowly headed east. They didn’t take the old road.

Ed watched them from the top of a hill. Scar came and stood beside him.

“It’s not even a road yet,” Ed said.

“When people travel it, it will become one.”

“They said the opposite in Karsa.”

“I know.”

Not making a road was a choice too. Making a road was a choice too. What mattered was—

Ed didn’t say it. He had already written it in his notebook.

A thin trail of sand rose behind the wagons as they grew smaller.

Long ago, Hohenheim had gone east. After that, Scar’s brother had gone east too, by a different road. And come back. Ed had followed those traces too, and come back.

But the people leaving now weren’t walking anyone’s road as it was. It was a new road the people of the present had decided on.

Ed slung his bag over his shoulder.

“Are you going too?” Scar asked.

“No.”

“Then where?”

Ed looked east. “Al’s coming.”

He would be bringing a load of alkahestry research from Xing. What Ed hadn’t seen. What Al had seen. What Al had gotten wrong. What Ed had gotten wrong. They’d probably fight the moment they met. And then look into things again.

Ed patted the notebook in his bag. “I still want to know about Ishvala too.”

“Even though you did not find the answer?”

“That’s exactly why.”

Scar looked at Ed. “So in the end, nothing has changed.”

Ed laughed. “No.” This time he answered right away. “Something changed.”

“What?”

“Even when there’s something I don’t know…” Ed thought for a moment. “It doesn’t make me as mad as it used to.” And he added, “A little.”

“…”

“It’s fun.”

The wind blew. Sand flowed thinly across the road.

Ed watched the survey party heading east for a while.

The journey he had set out on to find answers was over. What remained wasn’t answers. A few questions.

And—

a road to carry those questions on to whatever came next.
