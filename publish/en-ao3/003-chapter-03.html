The next morning, Ed was at Scar’s front door from first thing.

More precisely, he’d already been there when Scar opened his eyes. When Scar opened the door, Ed was standing there. Bread in one hand. In the other, yesterday’s notebook.

“Morning.”

Scar looked at him. “Why do you have that?”

Ed glanced down at the notebook. “I borrowed it yesterday.”

“I did not lend it to you.”

“Then why did I have it?”

“Because you carried it off.”

“That’s what borrowing is.”

“Give it back.”

“After I’m done.”

Scar considered Ed for a moment, then stepped back. “Come in.”

“See, you’re reasonable.”

“I merely dislike the noise outside.”

Ed walked in as if it were nothing.

The notebook lay open on the table again. Ed took a bite of bread and turned the pages.

He’d read it to the end last night. Alchemy. Ishvalan prayer. Alkahestry, heard from someone in Xing. Between the diary-like notes, lines about a troublemaking younger brother kept turning up. And the words that stayed with him to the very last.

Ishvala = the Truth?

There was no way to tell what conclusion Scar’s brother had reached. Only the questions remained.

“Scar.”

“What.”

“The word at the end of the notebook. Karsa. Ever heard of it?”

“No. I do not know whether it is a person or a place.”

“Sounds like a person’s name.”

Ed closed the notebook. “I think I figured something out.”

“What.”

“That line from yesterday.”

Ed opened the notebook and pointed to Ishvala = the Truth?

“At first I figured it was just what they called their god. But after human transmutation, I looked at it again, and no. That’s not it.”

He set the bread down.

“What is the Truth?” Scar asked.

Ed didn’t answer. A white space. A place where there was nothing. And there—an enormous Gate. The pure-white being seated before it. The grinning face. A form identical to his own.

I am what you call the world. Or the universe. Or God. Or Truth. Or All. Or One.

And I am you.

Ed looked down at his right hand. It was plain flesh and bone now. Once, it had been steel. The price for opening that Gate. And in the end, he had given up the Gate itself.

“Fullmetal.”

At Scar’s voice, Ed looked up. “What.”

“Your face looks strange.”

“It’s always looked like this.”

“That is not what I meant.”

Ed said nothing for a moment. Then he looked at the notebook again. “Scar.”

“What.”

“Ishvala is the creator god, right?”

“That is what we believe.”

“The god who made the world.”

“Yes.”

Ed’s eyes dropped to the line again. Ishvala = the Truth? The world. God. The Gate. Himself.

He said slowly, “…Maybe.”

Scar waited.

“I think Ishvala is that guy.”

“That guy?”

Ed tapped the notebook. “The Truth.”

For a while, Scar said nothing. Ed waited for a reaction.

“Say something.”

“What?”

“I don’t know, be surprised.”

“Why?”

“I think I just figured out the identity of the god you believe in!”

Scar’s expression didn’t change. “Do you think reading a few records means you know everything we have believed?”

“I never said I know everything.”

“You said you had found its identity.”

“That’s just how I put it.”

“Then put it correctly.”

Ed’s eyebrow twitched. “Whatever.”

He pulled the notebook closer. “We can just check.”

“Check what?”

“Whether there are more records like it.”

Ed stood. “Other than that underground prayer hall, is there anywhere I can look at old records?”

Scar sighed. “There is.”

“Let’s go.”

“It is still morning.”

“So?”

“Eat a proper meal.”

Ed held up his bread. “I’m eating.”

“Is that a meal?”

“You sound like Al all of a sudden.”

The records hall stood on the outskirts of the rebuilt town. It wasn’t a new building. It was a stone structure that had been half destroyed in the war, repaired for use. Inside were religious texts, old family registries, and documents that had survived from before the war.

Not every record had survived. A good deal more had been lost. Hearing that, Ed went quiet for a moment.

What the Ishvalan Civil War had destroyed wasn’t only people. It was what people remembered. What they had written down. What had been handed down. All of it had vanished with them.

The old man who kept the records looked at Ed. “You are Amestrian.”

“Yes.”

The old man glanced at Scar. Scar said curtly, “I wish to see the old prayers.”

“Which ones?”

Ed cut in. “Ones about the door, or the world, or the self.”

The old man looked at him. “That is a wide range.”

“And about God.”

“Wider still.”

Ed shut his mouth. Scar said, “Old records about Ishvala will do.”

The old man studied the two of them a moment longer, then went inside.

Ed whispered, “You could’ve just said that from the start.”

“You spoke first.”

“So you could’ve cut in faster.”

“Why should I interrupt you?”

“Aren’t you here to help?”

“You followed me.”

“…We can’t talk to each other.”

There were more records than he’d expected. And they were more varied than he’d expected. Prayers. Passages for funerals. Stories told to children. Phrases copied from murals. Records with later priests’ interpretations added.

At first, Ed read them one at a time. Then he sped up.

The world is one, yet flows in countless forms.

He stopped. He marked it beside the line.

Next.

One who knows oneself knows a part of the world.

Marked again. Next.

The place to which all life returns.

Again. Next.

The one who stood before the door saw the world.

And the world saw him.

Ed’s hand halted for an instant. Then he marked that too. Next.

The earth does not stand still.

Life rises from the earth and returns to the earth.

He didn’t mark this one. It seemed to have nothing to do with the world or God.

Ed’s expression grew brighter and brighter. “Look.”

Scar was reading a different record. “At what?”

“This.”

Ed held out a page. “The world is one.”

Another. “One who knows oneself knows a part of the world.”

Another. “The place to which all life returns.”

He counted off on his fingers. “The world. One. Self. God.”

Scar said, “The word ‘god’ does not appear in the records you just read.”

“I’m talking about the whole picture.”

“A convenient method.”

“No, listen.”

Ed leaned forward. “You know what the thing I met said?”

Scar looked at him in silence.

“It called itself the world.”

Ed’s finger tapped the page. “The universe.” Tap. “God.” Tap. “Truth.” Tap. “One.” And finally, “Me.”

Scar’s gaze rested on Ed for a moment. “That is the being called the Truth?”

“Yeah.”

“Does it look like a person?”

Ed thought. “…It’s kind of like one.”

“That is vague.”

“It looked exactly like me.”

Scar narrowed his eyes. “Like you?”

“In shape, anyway.” Ed gestured at his own body. “When I first saw it, it had my shape. No face. No color.”

“A being like that called itself a god.”

“It’s a god and it isn’t.”

“What does that mean?”

“I don’t know.”

Ed fell silent. The Truth had called itself God. But it wasn’t the god Ed had imagined. It didn’t answer prayers. It didn’t save humans. It didn’t seem to judge good or evil.

It simply was. And when a human opened the Gate, it was there.

Ed looked at the records again. “Still, it’s too similar.”

In the afternoon, the two of them found one more old place of prayer. It was much smaller than the underground one discovered the day before. Most of the roof had collapsed, and only one wall remained.

Scar said, “This place has been known for a long time.”

Ed examined the wall. At first there seemed to be nothing special. The sun. The earth. People. Animals. Trees. Shapes you’d commonly see in old Ishvalan religious art.

Then—

“Wait.”

Ed stepped up to the wall. There was a broken relief. A single figure stood there. In front of it, a tall vertical rectangle, a structure that looked like a door. And beyond it, a humanlike form.

Ed’s eyes widened. “…Hey.”

Scar came up beside him. “Look at this.”

“I am looking.”

“It’s a door.”

“It appears to be.”

“And there’s a person on this side.” Ed pointed to the figure beyond the door. “What’s this?”

“I do not know.”

Ed brought his face closer. Faint letters remained beneath the relief. Scar carefully brushed away the dust.

“Can you read it?”

“Parts.”

“What does it say?”

Scar studied the script for a moment, then read it slowly.

“One who sees Ishvala…”

The middle was broken. After that.

“…sees oneself.”

Ed didn’t move. Wind passed through the broken wall.

“Again.”

“One who sees Ishvala sees oneself.”

Ed’s heart beat a little faster. I am you. What the Truth had said.

He looked at the relief again. The person. The door. The being beyond it. And—himself.

“This is it.”

“What is?”

“The Truth.”

Ed’s voice quickened. “The one who stood before the door saw the world.”

His finger followed the relief. “The one who saw the world saw himself.”

Again, the being beyond the door. “The one who saw Ishvala also sees himself.”

Ed turned to Scar. “It’s the same.”

Scar studied the relief for a long moment. “They resemble each other.”

“More than resemble.”

“We do not know that yet.”

“What else do I need to see?”

Scar looked at him. “Something other than what you want to see.”

Ed’s mouth stopped. “What?”

“It is nothing.”

“What did you just—”

Scar had already walked off. “Hey!”

By sunset, Ed was back at Scar’s house. On the table lay lines copied out all day.

The one who stood before the door saw the world.

And the world saw him.

The world is one, yet flows in countless forms.

One who knows oneself knows a part of the world.

The place to which all life returns.

And—

One who sees Ishvala sees oneself.

Ed sat in the middle of it. “Isn’t this enough?”

Scar was drinking water. “For what?”

“To say Ishvala is the Truth.”

“No.”

“Why not?!”

“There are merely similar lines.”

“There was a door.”

“It looked like a door.”

“And something beyond it that looked like a person.”

“It looked like a person.”

“And it said you see yourself!”

“That part is correct.”

“Then we’re done!”

Scar set down the cup. “Fullmetal.”

“What.”

“You said you saw this Truth with your own eyes.”

“Yeah.”

“Do you therefore believe you understand it better than others?”

Ed answered at once. “Of course.”

Scar’s eyes narrowed slightly.

“I saw it myself.”

Ed picked up his notebook. “Your records have a door, a world, a god, and a self.” He lifted a pen. “That’s what I saw, too.”

There was yesterday’s line.

What is Ishvala?

He brought the pen beneath it. Scar said, “Do you think reading a few records means you know everything we have believed?”

“I didn’t say I know everything.”

“Then what?”

Ed thought for a moment. Then he smiled. “That I think I found one answer.”

The pen moved.

What is Ishvala?

Beneath it.

The Truth.

Ed looked at the two words. They fit strangely well. The door. The world. The One. God. The self. It was all so close to what he had seen with his own eyes. So there didn’t seem to be much reason to doubt it.

Ed put the pen down. “Found it.”

It had been simpler than he’d expected.

Scar still said nothing.
