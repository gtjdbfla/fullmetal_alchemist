Central, Amestris.

The fifth day since Graus’s trial began.

Mustang had three sheets of paper spread across the desk in the Fuhrer’s office.

The first. The powers of the Fuhrer.

The second. The powers of the supreme military commander.

The third. The empty space left when the two are separated.

Captain Hawkeye picked up the third sheet. “There are a lot of blanks.”

“There sure are.”

“I thought you had a plan.”

“I do.”

“What plan?”

Mustang pointed at the paper. “Filling in the blanks.”

Hawkeye looked at him for a moment. “Usually that is called not having a plan.”

“You’ve gotten talkative lately, Captain.”

“I wonder who I learned it from.”

Mustang laughed. Only for a moment.

He looked at the first sheet again. The Fuhrer. Highest authority in the nation. Supreme military commander. Appointment and dismissal of senior officers. Control of the major administrative bodies. Special powers in a national emergency.

Bradley had held these powers too. So did he. Only the person had changed. The seat was the same.

He remembered Brandt’s voice. Do not replace the person. Change the system. And Graus. Did you not do the same?

Mustang picked up his pen. He drew a line through the first entry.

Highest authority in the nation = supreme military commander

“Let’s separate this first.”

Hawkeye looked down at the paper. “You really mean to do it.”

“I do.”

“There will be a lot of opposition.”

“I know that too.”

“From the military as well.”

“Especially from the military.” Mustang got up from his chair. “So I’d better call the military in first.”

That afternoon, the generals gathered in a meeting room in Central.

General Olivier Mira Armstrong sat with her arms crossed. When Mustang came in, only her eyes moved. “The Fuhrer calling all the generals at once.”

“Busy?”

“If it were your funeral, I’d make the time.”

“Today’s something a little different.”

Mustang didn’t take his seat. He stood at the front of the room. “I intend to change the Fuhrer system.”

Silence. Someone coughed. Olivier didn’t so much as blink.

Mustang went on. “More precisely, I’m going to end the structure in which the Fuhrer commands both the nation and the military at the same time.”

This time there was definitely a murmur.

“Your Excellency.” An officer raised his hand. “Do you mean you’ll give up supreme command of the military?”

“The goal isn’t to reduce my own personal powers.”

“Then what is it?”

“To make sure the next person can’t have them either.”

The murmuring stopped.

“Supreme military command and supreme political power in the nation will be separated,” Mustang said. “The military budget will be reviewed by a civilian political body. The appointment of senior officers won’t end with one Fuhrer’s judgment either. And I’m going to fix the structure where units move in a commander’s personal name.”

Olivier snorted. “You mean Graus’s battalion. I hear those soldiers are still waiting on that name alone.”

“Your Excellency,” one general said quietly. “My unit knows my name too.”

The meeting room went quiet.

“I know,” Mustang said. “That’s why I’m saying this today.”

“And if we oppose it?”

“Then oppose it with your mouths. As long as you do it with your mouths, go ahead.”

Olivier muttered, “And if someone moves like Graus?”

“That’s exactly what I’m trying to prevent.” Mustang went on. “I’m also going to fix the structure where the military investigates incidents inside the military and reaches its own conclusions.”

One general frowned. “You mean civilians will control the military?”

“Yes.”

“People who don’t know war?”

Mustang looked at him. “And how did it turn out when we left it to the people who know war?”

No one answered. There was no need to say the name Ishval.

Mustang went on. “I’m not saying civilians will command military operations. I’m saying the military won’t get to decide the purpose of the nation too.”

“Is there a difference?” This time it was Olivier.

Mustang looked at her. “A big one.”

“Explain.”

“Soldiers can judge where to attack,” Mustang said. “But they can’t be the only ones who decide why we fight.”

Olivier’s eyes narrowed. “You’re only realizing that now?”

“Yes.”

Several officers in the room looked back and forth between the two of them in surprise.

Olivier snorted. “Late.”

“So I’m doing it fast.”

The opposition came faster than expected.

“It will weaken the military.”

“Responses to national emergencies will be slower.”

“Have you considered that politicians may use the military?”

Mustang nodded at most of it. “You’re right.”

One general stopped mid-sentence. “…Sir?”

“All of those are problems that could happen.”

“And you’re still pushing ahead?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Mustang thought for a moment. “Because I don’t think I can make a perfect system.”

Olivier looked at him.

“Until now, this country thought the opposite,” Mustang said. “A strong Fuhrer. A capable Fuhrer. A right Fuhrer. That if you put one person like that at the top, the country would run properly. Bradley was strong.” He paused for a moment. “And I sat down in this seat believing I was right.”

Someone lowered their eyes.

“The problem is the next one,” Mustang said. “Is there any guarantee the next Fuhrer will be better than me?”

No one answered.

“There isn’t. So the next one will believe he’s right too. And the one after that.” Mustang looked around at the generals. “I don’t want to hand the country over trusting in that anymore.”

After the meeting, Olivier stayed behind. Hawkeye stood by the door.

“So,” Olivier asked. “You mean to be the last Fuhrer?”

Mustang raised his head. “I suppose that’s how it’ll go.”

“You’re not making up some grand excuse because you want to step down, are you?”

“Do you know how hard I worked to sit in this chair?”

“That’s what makes it more suspicious.”

Mustang laughed. Olivier didn’t.

“What will you do about the military?”

“There’ll be a separate supreme military commander.”

“Who appoints them?”

“First I have to build a structure where I’m not the one who decides that alone.”

“And the civilian government?”

“Strengthen the role of the legislature, and separate the head of the executive from the military.”

“The judiciary?”

“Start with whatever can be pulled away from the military.”

Olivier uncrossed her arms. “State Alchemists?”

Mustang paused for a moment. “Them too.”

Hawkeye’s eyes moved.

“Human weapons the military takes out whenever it needs them,” Mustang said. “I’m not going to leave that structure as it is.”

“All at once?” Olivier asked.

“No.” Mustang shook his head. “If I did that, it’d collapse.”

“At least you know that.”

“I’ll take them apart one at a time.”

“How long will it take?”

“I don’t know.”

The corner of Olivier’s mouth rose very slightly. “The Fuhrer says ‘I don’t know’ quite easily.”

“It’s in fashion these days.”

“Who did you learn it from?”

Mustang didn’t answer.

The change didn’t begin with a declaration. It began with documents.

A bill to move part of the military budget under civilian review. A proposal to bring outside review into senior officer appointments. A proposal to narrow the scope of courts-martial. A proposal to take powers away from military commanders in local administration. A proposal to give Ishvalan residents’ representatives voting rights on the Ishval reconstruction committee.

Write one, and two problems appeared. Fix two, and five documents of opposition came in.

On the third day, Mustang slumped over his desk. “Maybe I should just be a dictator.”

Hawkeye turned a page. “That would be convenient for you.”

“Right?”

“Though I do not think that is why you started all this.”

Mustang turned only his face. “You won’t even let me joke.”

“The Fuhrer’s jokes sometimes sound like orders.”

Mustang sat up. That remark caught on something. “That’s a problem too.”

“What is?”

“That even my jokes have power.”

Hawkeye didn’t answer.

Mustang picked up his pen. “I’ll have to cut that down too.”

“Your jokes?”

“My power.”

A few days later, Mustang attended a small meeting. The only soldiers were him and Hawkeye. The rest were administrators, lawyers, and regional representatives.

There was one empty chair. The kind of seat Brandt would have taken.

Hawkeye set a folder on that chair. “It was on the desk in his quarters in Ishval.”

Mustang opened it. Civilian review of the military budget. Voting rights for regional representatives. Scope of courts-martial. In the margin of every item were dry notes.

The Fuhrer will definitely get angry at this item.

Colonel Miles will welcome this.

This will take the longest. Do it anyway.

At the very bottom of the last page was a single line.

Saying “don’t replace the person” is easy. Changing the system is what takes long.

Mustang couldn’t close the folder for a long time.

The meeting began.

“If you abolish the current structure as it is, there will be a power vacuum.”

“Then let’s hand it over in stages.”

“Separating military command from the executive means changing the entire constitutional structure.”

“That’s exactly what we’re saying we should change.”

“Your Excellency,” a lawyer asked carefully. “What kind of nation do you have in mind, in the end?”

Mustang started to answer, then stopped. A republic. A legislature. A civilian government. Several words came to mind. But he didn’t want to settle on any one of them as the answer now. He thought of Graus. He thought of Brandt. He thought of Ishval.

“A country that I don’t decide.”

The meeting room went quiet.

Mustang snorted. “I suppose that sounds too irresponsible, put that way.”

No one laughed.

“More precisely,” he corrected himself, “I don’t want to decide the shape of the next country by myself.”

This time he spoke formally. It wasn’t a military order.

“Let’s start by deciding where the military has to step out,” Mustang said. “After that, the people who come into those places should decide together.”

A regional representative asked, “How far do you intend to step back, Your Excellency?”

Mustang thought for a moment. “All the way.”

That night, only Mustang and Hawkeye were left in the office. The lights of Central could be seen through the window.

“Captain.”

“Sir.”

“Do you remember when I said I’d become Fuhrer?”

“I remember.”

“Did you regret it?”

Hawkeye thought for a moment. “A few times.”

“A few?”

“More times than I can count.”

“That’s not ‘a few.’”

“I suppose not.”

Mustang laughed.

After a moment, Hawkeye added, “Sir.”

“Hm.”

“I do not regret firing from the tower. But I was able to find that man because I was that kind of person.”

Mustang didn’t answer.

“I would like it to be a country that does not need people who know how to do that kind of thing.”

Mustang nodded, then looked out the window. “I thought if I sat in this seat, I could change things.”

“You have changed things.”

“Yes.” Mustang nodded. “And that seems more dangerous.”

Hawkeye looked at him.

“Because you can do good things,” he said. “And then you start believing this seat is necessary.”

Bradley had used this seat. So had he. In different directions. That didn’t mean the seat was different.

“The next one will think so too,” Mustang said. “That he’ll do better than me.”

Hawkeye was silent for a while. “Then,” she asked, “do you truly mean to be the last?”

Mustang went back to the desk. The Fuhrer’s seal lay there. Once, having it had been his goal. Mustang took the seal in his hand. It was heavy. He held it longer than he meant to. Then he put it down.

“Yes.” It was a short answer. “After me,” Mustang said, “there shouldn’t be a Fuhrer.”

Hawkeye nodded. “Understood.”

“That’s it?”

“What were you expecting?”

“Something moving.”

“It seems there will be a lot of work.”

Mustang scowled, then finally laughed.

On the desk, documents he hadn’t gotten to yet were piled up like a mountain. The Fuhrer’s seal was still there. He would need it tomorrow too. Probably for a while yet.

Mustang didn’t throw it away right then. Because there was one last thing to do. Build a country that could run without that seal.

That was the work left to the last Fuhrer.
