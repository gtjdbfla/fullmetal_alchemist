The next morning, Ed was about to leave Scar’s house when a voice came from behind him.

“Where are you going?”

“The prayer hall.”

“It is too early.”

Ed turned. “The sun’s up.”

“The caretaker has not arrived.”

“I’m just going to take a look.”

“You tried to touch it yesterday.”

“I didn’t touch it.”

“Because I stopped you.”

Ed pouted. “Why does looking at one stone take so much paperwork?”

Scar put on his shoes. “So wait.”

“You’re coming?”

“It is better than you going alone.”

“I’m not a kid.”

Scar looked down at him. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“You just checked my height.”

“I said nothing.”

“Your eyes said it!”

The old man from before was already waiting in the underground prayer hall. Once he had permission, Ed headed straight for the floor.

The circular pattern. The lines linking people, earth, trees, and animals. And the dark red stones set where the lines met.

Today he looked at the positions of the stones rather than the pattern itself. He opened his notebook. One. Two. Three. He transferred the stone positions onto the page as points. After marking a handful, his expression turned serious.

“I knew it.”

Scar asked, “What?”

“They aren’t just anywhere.”

Ed pointed to the pattern. “They’re all where the lines meet.”

Two lines. Three lines. One at the center, where the most lines converged.

“If they were decoration, they wouldn’t need to be placed like this.”

The old man said, “They may be for prayer.”

“Could be,” Ed agreed readily. Then added right away, “But I should still check.”

Scar looked at Ed’s hand. “What are you going to do?”

“If I just take one out—”

“No.”

“I haven’t taken it yet!”

“You were going to.”

“I only wanted to see if it moves a little—”

“No.”

Ed looked at the old man. The old man shook his head too. Ed pulled his hand back.

“Fine.”

After a moment: “Has anyone already taken one out?”

The old man thought. “Not here.”

“Other places?”

“I do not know.”

Ed looked at the stones again. He remembered the line he’d read yesterday.

The flow may be borrowed.

The lines of the prayer hall. The dark stones set at their crossings. And an ancient record with no word for price. The hypothesis he’d voiced last night lifted its head again.

“What I said yesterday,” Scar said. “The price of the flow.”

“Yeah. If these stones are that.”

“On what basis?”

“I’m looking for it now.”

“That is not a basis.”

“That’s why I said I’m looking.”

Ed crouched. “Amestrian alchemists paid a price for opening the Gate.”

“Yes.”

“But the old Ishvalan records don’t have that.”

“Yes.”

“Instead, there are stones like these.”

Scar’s eyes narrowed slightly. “You are saying the stones substitute for the price.”

Ed stopped. “…One possibility.”

“You have been saying the same thing since last night.”

“That’s how hypotheses start.”

First, though, they needed to examine the stone more closely.

The lead came, unexpectedly, from Miles.

After they came out of the prayer hall and returned to town, Miles called to Ed. “Is this what you were looking for?”

On his desk lay a small scrap of cloth. And on it, a dark red stone.

Ed lunged at it. “Where’d you get it?”

“A water pipe construction site.”

“It’s not one you pulled out of the prayer hall, right?”

“No.” Miles said, “It turned up while digging.”

Ed picked up the stone. Its color was close to the one set in the prayer hall floor. Dark red. A rough surface. About the size of a fingertip.

“Same thing?”

“A local elder said it resembles the stones he used to see in prayer halls.”

“What’s it made of?”

“We do not know.”

“If I analyze it with alchemy—”

Ed stopped mid-sentence. Miles looked at him. Ed looked at Miles.

“What?”

“You were going to do it yourself?”

“…”

Ed set the stone down. “I’ll just get someone.”

The corner of Miles’s mouth rose, very slightly. “A good idea.”

“Don’t laugh.”

Soon after, a military alchemist who’d been assisting with the reconstruction was called in. A young man. When he recognized Ed, his eyes went wide.

“The Fullmetal Alchemist?”

Ed’s face crumpled. “Former.”

“Oh, I’m sorry.”

“Just call me Ed.”

The man grew more flustered. Seeing the stone on the table, he asked, “Should I analyze this?”

“As far as you can.”

Ed folded his arms. “Start with the structure.”

The alchemist placed the stone on a prepared transmutation circle. Ed naturally took a step back. The transmutation began. Light flashed. Part of the stone’s surface cracked. Ed was beside it at once.

“Well?”

“No particular reaction.”

“Any energy amplification?”

“None.”

“Any strange interference during the transmutation?”

“Nothing like that either.”

Ed’s expression stiffened. “Again.”

A second test. The result was the same. For the third, they applied heat.

This time the alchemist tilted his head. “That’s odd.”

“What is?”

“It doesn’t change easily.”

“Meaning what?”

“It stands up to heat quite well.”

Ed peered at the fragment. “How well?”

“It’s hard to measure precisely with the equipment here.”

“Pressure?”

“That would need to be looked at separately.”

Miles cut in. “This will have to go to Central for a detailed analysis.”

Ed looked down at the stone. None of the reactions he’d hoped for had occurred. It didn’t amplify the flow. It didn’t strengthen alchemy. At least within what they’d verified so far, it didn’t look like any special energy source.

Ed muttered, “Maybe not.”

Scar asked, “What?”

“The thing that pays in place of the price.”

“The stone?”

“Yeah.”

Ed thought for a moment, then shook his head. “I still don’t know.”

The alchemist asked cautiously, “But you’re not doing it yourself?”

Ed looked at him. “I can’t.”

“Pardon?”

“Alchemy.”

The man’s face stiffened. “Oh…”

“Don’t make that face.”

“What face?”

“The one you’re making now.”

“I’m sorry.”

“And don’t apologize.”

Scar said, “You are particular.”

“You stay out of this.”

In the afternoon, Miles brought an old military document.

“There is a record of a similar mineral.”

Ed took it. “Where from?”

“The military’s mineral files. Apparently it was once used in special alloy research.”

“This stone?”

“If it’s the same type.”

Ed read quickly. Output: negligible. Refinement difficulty: high. It wasn’t a mineral you could use in bulk, like iron or copper. But there was a record that when mixed with certain metals, it had greatly improved resistance to heat and pressure.

“Alloys.”

“Yes.”

“What did they use it for?”

“There are records of experimental use in high-temperature equipment and pressure components. There may have been military research too, but the files here don’t say in detail.”

“Expensive?”

“Very.”

Ed looked up. “Why?”

“Because it’s rare.”

A simple answer. Ed looked at the stone in his hand. A little while ago, he’d thought it was the key to unlocking the secrets of ancient Ishval. But it might be something else entirely.

“Does it turn up anywhere besides Ishval?”

“According to the files, a few places.”

“A lot?”

“No.” Miles pointed at the document. “Mostly in small amounts.”

Ed sank into thought. “Then if this really is the same mineral…”

“We will not know until Central confirms it.”

“I know.”

He admitted it surprisingly easily. Miles looked at him. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“Don’t you start too.”

From that day on, Ed went around the reconstruction sites, looking for places where the stone had turned up. The first was the water pipe site. The second, where an old well was being redug. The third, where a road was being widened. And the fourth, beneath a collapsed house.

He couldn’t be certain they were all the same stone. But the color and the shape were similar. Ed marked the locations on a map.

By evening, Scar looked down at the map. “There are many.”

“More than I expected.”

Ed spread out a sheet on which he’d copied a drawing from an old Ishvalan record. Lines presumed to represent the flow of the earth. He compared them with the map he’d marked, back and forth.

“…Don’t they look alike?”

Scar said, “Which part?”

“The direction.”

The points where the stones had turned up didn’t match exactly. But roughly, they seemed to run in the same direction as the lines in the old record. Ed’s eyes lit up.

“No way, it’s actually—”

He stopped. Scar waited. Ed shut his mouth. A moment later, he said, “We don’t know the order.”

“Of what?”

“Whether those people sensed the flow because of this stone.”

Ed tapped the map. “Or whether stones like this naturally form together under the same geological conditions.”

After a pause. “Or they might have nothing to do with each other.”

Scar said, “That too.”

Ed looked at him. “What?”

“You seem to have learned a little.”

“Why is everyone telling me that lately?”

Scar didn’t answer. Ed looked at the map again.

There was a decisive counterexample. Xing. The alkahestrists read the Dragon’s Pulse. But he’d never heard that they needed stones like these. May didn’t use one. In that case, it was hard to say this stone was a material that created the world’s flow itself.

“No,” Ed muttered.

“What?”

“At least it doesn’t seem to be the cause of the flow.”

Scar said nothing. One hypothesis was gone. Yet strangely, the problem was only getting bigger.

A few days later, a worker digging on the edge of town called people over. Ed and Scar went to the site too.

Beneath the ground that had been cut away for a road foundation, a dark red layer had been exposed. It was unlike the small stones they’d seen at first. A long, dark red seam ran through the rock.

Ed crouched. “Is this all…?”

Miles said, “We do not know yet.”

“I know.” Ed answered at once. “You can’t call it a vein when no one’s even surveyed it.” He brushed the dirt away with his hand. “And we don’t know how far it runs.”

Miles nodded. “We will need a geological survey team.”

A few of the old Ishvalans nearby didn’t look surprised. Ed noticed.

“You knew about this?”

One old man said, “The stone itself.”

“That there’s a lot of it here?”

“We would see it in the ground sometimes.”

“Then why didn’t you dig it up?”

The old man studied Ed for a moment, as if he didn’t understand the question. Ed turned to Scar. “You knew too?”

“I saw it as a child.”

“Like this?”

“Around places of prayer.”

Ed looked at the layer again. “Then why isn’t there a mine?”

Scar said, “It was not a stone to be dug up.”

Ed shut his mouth. That single sentence lingered strangely. In Amestris, when someone found a useful stone, the first thing they’d think was: What can it be used for? How much is there? How much can it sell for? How do we dig it out?

But here, nobody thought that just because a stone was there, they ought to dig it.

Ed slowly stood. “Miles.”

“What?”

“If this really is the mineral in the Central files—”

“That is still an assumption.”

“I know. If.”

Ed looked at the layer. “And if there’s a ton of it here?”

Miles didn’t answer right away. He seemed to be thinking the same thing. A rare mineral. Expensive special alloys. Almost no output anywhere. And Ishval in the middle of its reconstruction.

Ed scratched his head. “…I think I found something troublesome.”

Scar said, “You did not find it.”

“I know.” Ed sighed. “But now I know about it.”

The stone said nothing. In the prayer hall, it had been set at the crossings of the lines for hundreds of years. In the ground, it might have lain there even longer.

Some people called it a sacred stone. Some called it a rare mineral. Soon, there would be some who called it money.

No one yet knew exactly how much of it there was, or how much it was worth. Or what the people who learned of it would do.
