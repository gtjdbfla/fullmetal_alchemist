It was Scar’s third day back in Ishval.

“We have to conduct a survey.”

At Adin’s words, the meeting room grew noisy again.

“I’m not saying we should build a mine. We need to know where it is and how much there is before we can decide what to do.”

An old man opposite him struck the floor with his cane. “Once the digging starts, who will stop what comes next?”

“We’re already digging to lay water pipes and build houses.”

“That is not the same!”

“Which is why I’m saying we should find out exactly how it’s different.”

Someone at the back cut in. “How are we supposed to trust survey results from Amestris?”

“Then who will do the survey?”

“We can bring in our own people.”

“With whose money?”

Voices overlapped.

Leaning against the wall, Scar said nothing. Before leaving for Karsa, he would have searched for an answer himself. His brother’s records. The ancient Ishvalan prayer grounds. The stone found there. He would have thought that discovering what those things were would also tell him what to do next.

But what he’d seen in Karsa had not been a single answer. Someone who believed closing the roads was the only way to survive. Someone who believed it was time to reconsider the roads. Both spoke for the same city.

Beside him, Miles said quietly, “You picked a good time to return.”

“Is it like this every day?”

“Only the subject changes.”

“Yesterday?”

“The wells.”

“The day before?”

“The school site.”

Scar looked ahead. “Is this what reconstruction is?”

“It would have been easy if it only meant putting buildings back up,” Miles said. “Making somewhere for people to live again seems to be another matter.”

In the end, Miles prohibited any separate excavation until the survey team arrived. Reconstruction would continue. If the stone appeared during construction, only its location would be recorded. No one was to dig it out on their own.

After the meeting, Adin followed Scar.

“What was Karsa like?”

“Why?”

“You went there.”

Scar looked at Miles. Miles suddenly looked down at the papers in his hand.

“People were living there.”

“That’s all?”

“What else do you want to hear?”

Adin thought for a moment. “Are they living well?”

“They are living.”

“I know that much.”

“Then?”

“Do they trade with the outside?”

“Very little.”

“Why?”

“They chose it.”

Adin fell silent for a moment. “And are they living well?”

This time, Scar didn’t answer at once. He remembered Karsa’s old channels. Narrow fields. Stone houses. Erased roads. What they had protected, and what it had cost them.

“That, too,” Scar said, “is something the people there must keep deciding.”

Adin gave him an odd look. “I thought you went looking for an answer.”

“I found one.”

“But you look like you know even less.”

“The answer I found is not yours.”

Adin gave a small laugh. “Travel’s made you even harder to get an answer out of.”

“So it seems.”

That same day, in Central, three kinds of documents lay on Erich Brandt’s desk. An Ishval reconstruction progress report. A preliminary analysis of a substance believed to be a rare mineral. And a report on residents’ opinions.

The red mineral matched Crimstone from military inventory records in many respects. A mineral used in tiny amounts for weapons materials, with few known sources, all small.

Detailed analysis was still pending. Deposit size unknown. Economic viability unknown.

Yet the words Central used to describe Ishval were already multiplying. Reconstruction. Public order. Resources. Economy. Security.

Brandt gathered the papers and headed to the Fuhrer’s office.

Mustang received him from behind his desk. Brandt sat opposite him and slid over the residents’ statements.

“That is why an independent review is necessary.”

“Miles’s reports aren’t enough?”

“I am not saying Colonel Miles cannot be trusted.”

“Then?”

“The problem is that you can trust him so well.”

Mustang narrowed his eyes. “An odd compliment.”

“I am talking about the structure.”

Brandt counted on his fingers. “Colonel Miles manages the situation on the ground. He reports to the Fuhrer. The Fuhrer decides.”

“So far.”

“Which is why I am proposing a change.”

“Miles keeps consulting the local residents.”

“I know.”

“They’ll take part in the survey too.”

“I know that as well.”

“And you still want to send someone else?”

“Sending one more person selected by the Fuhrer alone would accomplish nothing.”

A brief silence.

“You’re choosing your words particularly sweetly today,” Mustang said.

“If Crimstone really is an economically viable resource, Ishval is no longer solely a reconstruction issue.”

Brandt didn’t back down.

“Funding will follow. Companies will follow. The military will follow. Will it still be enough for a local official the Fuhrer trusts to report, and the Fuhrer to decide?”

“You think I’ll do whatever I like with Ishval?”

“No.”

The answer came quickly. “That is why I am raising this now.”

Mustang looked at him. “What does that mean?”

“I know you will not act arbitrarily,” Brandt said. “But institutions must not be built on the assumption of individual goodwill.”

This time, Mustang didn’t answer at once.

“Today, the Fuhrer has decided to ask the people of Ishval.”

“Yes.”

“The next Fuhrer may decide not to.”

Brandt asked, “What do the people of Ishval possess now? A right?”

“…”

“Or an opportunity Roy Mustang has permitted them?”

Mustang’s eyes dropped to the documents on his desk. After a while, he asked, “So who do you propose?”

Brandt took out a personnel file he had prepared.

“Brigadier General Graus.”

Mustang’s hand stopped. “Graus?”

“He served in Ishval. He also has ample field command experience.”

“His rank is too high.”

“That is an advantage. He can operate alongside Colonel Miles while reporting independently to Central.”

“Two command structures on the ground would be more dangerous.”

“Leave reconstruction and public order under Colonel Miles,” Brandt said. “Brigadier General Graus will only investigate and assess.”

Mustang turned the pages of the file. Early thirties. Brigadier general. Excellent military academy record. Service in Ishval. Extensive field command experience. Rapid promotion during the postwar military reorganization.

“He rose quickly.”

“Even after you took office.”

“Because he was capable.”

Brandt nodded. “All the better, then.”

Mustang looked at him. “And he isn’t one of my people.”

A brief silence.

“Correct.”

“Does that matter?”

“Very much.”

Mustang gave a dry laugh. “Honest.”

“Sending a close associate of yours would defeat the purpose of an independent review.”

Mustang studied the file for a long time. Then he picked up his pen.

“Field command remains with Miles.”

“Agreed.”

“Graus will head a special investigation for Central. An independent assessment of the Crimstone issue’s political and public-order implications.”

“Good.”

“He has no authority to expand the investigation without consulting the residents.”

Brandt nodded. Mustang signed.

“Satisfied?”

“For the moment.”

“What an unpleasant answer.”

That afternoon, Graus entered Brandt’s office. He looked young for the insignia on his uniform.

“Brigadier General Graus.”

“Assemblyman Brandt.”

They shook hands.

“I recommended you.”

Graus’s gaze changed slightly. “You did?”

“Yes.”

“May I ask why?”

“I hear you are capable.”

“That cannot be the only reason.”

Brandt watched him for a moment. “You are not one of the Fuhrer’s people.”

The corner of Graus’s mouth moved very slightly. “A compliment?”

“A condition.”

Brandt pushed the residents’ statements toward him. “Have you read these?”

“Yes.”

“What do you make of them?”

“Opinions are divided.”

“Is that dangerous?”

“The present material is not sufficient to judge.”

“Some people are strongly critical of the government.”

“That alone is no basis for judging them dangerous.”

Brandt looked up. “Unexpected.”

“What is?”

“To hear that from an officer who spent so long under Bradley.”

Graus’s expression didn’t change.

“Arresting people merely for criticizing the government was not military regulation.”

After a moment, he added, “Some people simply did not follow the regulations.”

Brandt nodded slowly. The man differed little from the personnel record. Composure. Accurate assessment. Duty placed above personal connections.

“Good,” Brandt said. “That is the perspective I want you to bring.”

“What exactly do you want?”

“Show me Ishval’s instability.”

Graus’s gaze stopped. “Do you want a conclusion that it is unstable?”

Brandt reconsidered his words.

“I misspoke. If it is unstable, show me. If it is stable, report that it is stable.”

“Do you not wish to keep the Fuhrer in check?”

“I do. But I do not wish to alter the facts.”

“A finding of stability would favor the Fuhrer.”

“Then that is what you must write.”

Graus watched Brandt for a moment, then nodded.

Brandt asked, “Ishval was your first combat deployment. You were a lieutenant.”

Something vanished from Graus’s expression. “You have done your research.”

“Fuhrer Mustang was there too. A major, was he not?”

“Yes.”

“Not much older than you.”

“About a year.”

Nine years ago, a major and a lieutenant. Now, a Fuhrer and a brigadier general. Graus, too, had risen quickly, but side by side, the distance looked considerable.

“Do you know each other well?”

“No.”

The answer was a little too quick. Brandt didn’t pursue it.

“Report the facts.”

“Understood.”

After Graus left, Brandt looked at the file again. Ishval service. Rapid promotion. Not a close associate of Mustang. For now, he didn’t look like the wrong choice.

The next afternoon, a military train pulled into Ishval Station. Miles waited on the platform. The door opened, and Graus stepped down.

“General.”

“Colonel Miles.”

They exchanged brief salutes.

“I did not expect you to come personally.”

“It seems they sent me to see things for myself.”

Outside the station lay a city under reconstruction. New houses. Water pipes. A temporary school. Carts loaded with materials. Ishvalans carrying bricks beside Amestrian soldiers. Children running in the distance.

Graus slowed slightly. “It has changed a great deal.”

“Your first visit since the war?”

“Yes.”

Miles glanced at him. “Have you been informed about field command?”

“It is yours.”

“And the investigation team?”

“Mine.”

“Do I report to you, then?”

Graus shook his head. “Carry on as you have been.”

“And you, sir?”

“I was told to look independently.”

“By whom?”

Graus thought for a moment. “Both of them.”

A man approached from the edge of the reconstruction area. Sunglasses. A scar across his forehead.

Graus stopped.

Miles spoke first. “Scar.”

Graus’s gaze hardened. “…That man.”

“Yes.”

“What is he doing here?”

“Taking part in reconstruction.”

Scar looked at Graus too.

“The man who killed State Alchemists and soldiers,” Graus said.

“Yes.”

“General,” Miles said quietly.

Graus raised a hand to stop him. “I did not say I would arrest him.”

“Nor is what you said wrong,” Scar said.

Graus looked at him. “Some of the soldiers you killed were people I knew.”

“No doubt.”

Their eyes met for several seconds. Graus turned away first.

“Let us go.”

After they had walked a little farther, Miles said, “You let that pass more quietly than I expected.”

“Did you expect me to draw a gun?”

“Not quite that.”

“What Scar did has not disappeared,” Graus said. “But that does not allow us to punish him for things he is not doing now.”

Miles watched him for a moment. “I agree.”

That night, Graus wrote his first field report.

Political and public-order conditions in Ishval. Residents’ meetings ongoing. Conflicting opinions on the Crimstone survey. No organized violence to date. No direct clashes between military and residents. Scar, an Ishvalan responsible for past killings of State Alchemists and military personnel, currently participating in reconstruction.

After that last sentence, Graus added:

No current hostile acts observed.

And finally:

At this stage, public-order conditions in Ishval are assessed as stable.

His pen stopped. He remembered Brandt’s voice. If it is unstable, show me. If it is stable, report that it is stable.

Graus did not change the sentence. He signed it as it stood.

Ishval was stable now. At least as far as he had seen.

He pushed the report aside and opened the summary from Central.

Residents’ anxiety increasing following the discovery of Crimstone.

Below it were the residents’ statements. Support for the survey. Conditional support. Opposition. Protection of religious sites. Opposition to state ownership. Demands for jobs. Resident participation.

Graus looked from one document to the other. Some people were anxious. Some were hopeful. Some had no interest in the survey itself. Even those in favor had different reasons. So did those opposed.

But by the time they reached Central, they had all become the same person.

The residents.

And those residents were described as sharing one emotion.

Anxiety.

It wasn’t wrong. But it didn’t adequately describe what was happening on the ground, either.

Graus wrote in a corner of the report:

Original source material must be checked.

Distinctions between opinions lost during summarization.

His pen stopped. A single line in a report was turning different people into one.

Graus looked out the window. Lights still burned in some houses in nighttime Ishval. People were building walls. Some wanted to dig tomorrow. Some wanted that ground left untouched.

For now, that was all.

Graus checked the next day’s schedule. First survey area. Near the old prayer grounds. Seven in the morning.

He closed the papers.
