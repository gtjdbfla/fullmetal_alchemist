Ishval, Amestris.

The first shot rang out in the morning.

By the time Miles reached the scene, people were already packed along both sides of the alley.

“Please make way.”

The soldiers opened a path. A man lay on the ground. Thirty-eight. He had hauled lumber in the reconstruction zone.

Miles looked at the army doctor sitting beside him. The doctor shook his head without a word.

“Was he armed?”

“No, sir.”

“Any trouble with the army?”

“None confirmed.”

“The shooter?”

Again, there was no answer.

Miles stood up. When he looked around, soldiers were holding people back. Some already had their rifles raised. On the other side, Ishvalans glared at the soldiers.

“Lower your guns.”

“Colonel?”

“Lower them.”

“The shooter might still be nearby.”

Miles turned to the soldier. “Then who are you aiming at?”

The soldier shut his mouth.

“Lower them.”

One by one, the muzzles pointed down.

Miles turned toward the residents. “Did anyone see?”

No one answered. Instead, a voice came from the back. “People are saying the army shot him.”

“Who saw it?”

It was quiet.

“I am asking whether anyone saw it.”

Again, no answer.

Miles let out a short breath. “Then we do not know yet.”

“Soldiers are standing here holding guns. What don’t we know?”

“There are guns in Ishval too.”

People’s faces hardened.

Miles added at once, “Which means we do not know yet, either way.”

He held out his hand to his adjutant. “The report.”

He took the paper and wrote a few lines.

Civilian dead: 1.

His pen paused for a moment.

Shooter unidentified.

“Find witnesses first. And pull the army back one block.”

“Sir.”

Miles looked at the body again. One person had died. For now, that was all. Keeping it from becoming more than that was his job.

“He was a civilian.”

Graus looked up from the report. Bark stood in front of his desk.

“And unarmed.”

“Yes, sir.”

“What did I tell you?”

“You said to avoid civilians if possible.”

Graus’s brow furrowed. “Yes.”

“It was a case where it could not be avoided.”

“What does that mean?”

“Attacking a soldier would only tighten security inside the army. Attacking an armed Ishvalan would likely be classified as an internal clash.” Bark’s voice was the same as always. “I judged that a civilian death would raise distrust on both sides the fastest.”

Graus didn’t say anything for a while. “I never told you to kill civilians.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I told you to avoid them.”

“If possible.”

Graus’s eyes fixed on Bark. Bark didn’t look away.

It wasn’t wrong. Graus had said it himself. If possible. When you order someone to make a war, you can’t spell out every situation one by one. That was what he had thought. The field has to be judged by the commander in the field.

“…Be careful next time.”

“Understood.”

Bark saluted and went out. The door closed.

Graus looked at the report again. Civilian dead: 1. Below it was a more important sentence.

No further clashes.

Miles. Fast, as expected. He had pulled back his troops, met with the residents’ representatives, and eased the checkpoints. In less than a day, he had calmed things down.

Graus set the report down. He was a good commander. That was the problem.

Until the next morning, Ishval was quiet. On the surface, at least.

People closed up earlier than the day before, and stopped talking when they saw a soldier. Still, lines formed at the wells. The market opened. Army vehicles passed through on schedule.

Miles received the reports and nodded.

“If nothing happens today, it should settle down a little,” his adjutant said.

“I hope so,” Miles answered.

Two hours later, the second shot rang out.

This time it was a woman. She had sold cloth and household goods at the market.

By the time Miles arrived, the market was already closed. People had closed their shutters, and all that was left in the middle of the street was an overturned basket and a few pieces of cloth.

“Age?”

“Forty-two.”

“Armed?”

“No.”

Miles closed his eyes, then opened them. “The shooter.”

“We couldn’t find him.”

“Witnesses?”

“Lots of people heard the shot. Nobody saw who fired.”

Miles held out his hand to his adjutant. Yesterday’s report and today’s report lay side by side. A man. A woman. Different places. Different times. Both civilians. Both shooters unidentified.

“Isn’t it the same man?” his adjutant asked.

Miles shook his head. “Who knows.”

“Two days in a row.”

“We still do not know.”

“Do you think it’s a coincidence?”

Miles looked at him. “Did I say that?”

“No, sir.”

“Something being strange and knowing something are different.” Miles folded the two sheets. “Check it.”

Shouting came from outside. This time there were more people than yesterday.

“The army is killing people!”

“Where’s your proof?”

“Someone’s dead again!”

A few soldiers moved forward. Miles raised his hand. “That’s enough.”

“Colonel.”

“Pull back again today.”

“Again?”

“Again.”

“If we just keep pulling back—”

“Then what?”

The soldier shut his mouth.

“The reason those people are angry right now,” Miles said, “is that two people have died.” He pointed at the soldier’s rifle. “Do not make it three.”

The soldier slowly lowered his hands.

That afternoon, a few stones were thrown. One window broke. A soldier was grabbed by the collar. A resident was shoved to the ground by a soldier.

But no gun went off. By sunset, people had scattered again.

The second time too, Miles stopped it.

Graus looked down at the second report for a long time. This time, he called Bark in right away.

“Was it you?”

“Yes, sir.”

Graus set the report on the desk. “A civilian again.”

“You said ‘if possible,’” Bark said. “It has been effective so far.”

Graus’s face hardened. “Effective?”

“Residents’ distrust of the army has increased. Within the army, wariness toward local residents has risen as well.”

“And yet.” Graus tapped the last line of the report.

No large-scale clashes.

“Nothing has happened.”

“Colonel Miles is stabilizing the situation again.”

Graus leaned back in his chair. Two people had died. And still, Ishval hadn’t collapsed. It was tougher than he had expected. More precisely—Miles was holding it together better than he had expected.

“Do you think this will be enough?”

Bark thought for a moment. “No.”

Graus looked at him. The next question was simple. Then what will you do next? All he had to do was open his mouth. And if he didn’t like the answer, all he had to do was give an order.

Do not touch civilians anymore. Not “if possible.” Never.

Graus’s lips moved, then stopped.

Mustang hadn’t moved yet. Central still saw this as a local disturbance. If he stopped now—it would all end with two people dead. Nothing would change.

Graus asked, “Is there another way?”

“There is.”

“Its effectiveness?”

“Likely lower than now.”

“…”

Graus knew. He knew what he was asking right now. And what he wasn’t asking.

After a long while, he said, “Go.”

“Sir.”

Bark saluted. He walked to the door.

“Bark.”

Bark looked back.

Graus looked at him for a moment. All he had to do was say it. Even now.

“…Never mind.”

“Understood.”

The door closed. Graus was left alone.

Two reports lay on his desk. The first, a man. The second, a woman. Both civilians. Bark had killed them both.

Graus knew that. And he did not forbid a third.

The next day, around noon, people came out to the market in Ishval again. Fewer than the day before. But they came. They had to eat. They had to draw water. They had to rebuild their ruined houses. Life didn’t stop just because things were uncertain.

A few children were playing at the far end of the market. Miles had reduced the number of patrols.

“I think today will be all right,” his adjutant said.

Miles didn’t answer. He didn’t want to say “all right” yet. Still, it was quieter than yesterday.

That was when it happened.

The third shot rang out. Short. One round.

People turned their heads. And then—

A child fell.

For a moment, there was no sound at all. A ball slipped out of the child’s hand and rolled into the middle of the road.

“…Ah?”

Someone made a strange sound. Then came the screaming.

“The child!”

Someone ran over. It was Adin. “Yunus!”

The soldiers moved too. “Medic!”

But another voice burst out before that cry. “Again.”

Someone said, “The army killed another one.”

“No!” a soldier shouted. “We didn’t shoot!”

“Then who did?!”

“We don’t know!”

“That’s three!”

At those words, people stopped. Three. The first had been a man. The second had been a woman. And this time, a child.

“They’re killing us,” someone said. “One at a time.”

Miles pushed through the crowd. “Nothing has been confirmed yet!”

“What more is there to confirm?!”

“No one saw the shooter!”

“A child is dead!”

“That and who fired are separate—”

A stone flew. It hit a soldier in the shoulder. The soldiers moved.

“Do not raise your guns!” Miles shouted. “Everyone stay where you are!”

Adin burst out of the crowd. His face had gone white.

“They killed another one!” Adin shouted at the soldiers. “A man, a woman, and now the kid who kicked a ball around the school site every day! Amestris is doing it to us again—”

“Adin.”

A low voice cut him off. It was Zahir. The hand gripping Adin’s shoulder didn’t waver.

“Let go of me, Mr. Zahir!”

“If you bring down that soldier, the next ones to die will be our people.”

“So we’re just supposed to stand here?!”

“We still do not know who fired.”

Zahir looked at Miles without letting go of Adin. “Colonel. Pull back your troops. I will hold back our side.”

Adin shook off Zahir’s hand. But he only stood there with his fists clenched.

And then—

The fourth shot rang out.

Bang.

Miles’s head whipped around. A soldier went down.

In an instant, the soldiers’ muzzles came up.

“Gunfire!”

“Don’t shoot!” Miles shouted.

But someone on the other side raised a gun too.

Bang.

The fifth.

Bang.

The sixth.

After that, they couldn’t be counted.

People ran. Soldiers looked for cover. Residents dragged the wounded away. The voices asking who had fired first quickly disappeared. Now all that mattered was who was holding a gun in front of you.

“Cease fire!” Miles grabbed a soldier’s muzzle and forced it down.

“They fired from over there!”

“Who?!”

The soldier couldn’t answer.

Another shot rang out in the distance. This time it was a different street.

Miles’s face went rigid.

“Clash at the western checkpoint!” A messenger came running.

Another soldier ran up right behind him. “Shots fired on the southern road too!”

“Who fired?”

“We don’t know!”

Rumor was faster than gunfire.

A child is dead.

That one sentence raced through Ishval. In some places, they said the army had killed a child. In other places, they said Ishvalans had even sacrificed a child to attack the army. Neither was confirmed. Both moved people.

Before sundown, there was fighting at two checkpoints. Soldiers died. Ishvalan residents died too. An army vehicle burned.

No one had ordered it. It wasn’t Bark who had done it. Now people were looking at each other and pulling the trigger.

“What should we send to Central?”

Reports kept piling up on Miles’s desk. Wounded. Dead. Checkpoint clashes. Roads closed. More shots fired.

One sentence was already written at the top.

Civilian dead: 3. The third was a child.

Miles held his pen over that line for a long time.

Local unrest has escalated.

He crossed it out. He wrote again.

Armed clashes between military and residents have escalated.

Gunfire sounded outside. Miles’s pen stopped again. And at last, he wrote:

Fighting has broken out in multiple areas.

He didn’t write the word war. Not yet.

The report reached Graus.

Civilian dead: 3.

The third fatality was a child.

Subsequent fighting between military and residents.

Military dead.

Residents dead.

Clashes at two checkpoints.

Some roads beyond control.

Graus couldn’t take his eyes off the third line.

The third fatality was a child.

Yesterday’s scene came back to him.

“Do you think this will be enough?”

“No.”

And then.

“Is there another way?”

“There is.”

“Its effectiveness?”

“Likely lower than now.”

He hadn’t asked the next question. What Bark was going to do. Instead, he had sent him.

Graus closed his eyes. He hadn’t ordered him to kill a child. He hadn’t ordered him to kill civilians either. If anything, he had told him to avoid them.

If possible.

Those two words wouldn’t leave his head.

“Brigadier General,” an officer called carefully. “Second Lieutenant Bark has returned.”

Graus opened his eyes. “…Send him in.”

A moment later the door opened. Bark came in. “You called for me, sir.”

Graus looked at the reports on the desk. The first. A man. The second. A woman. The third. A child.

When the first shot rang out, he had given a warning. When the second shot rang out, he had known. And still he had not stopped it.

The third shot rang out.

The shots after that—

were no longer Bark’s.
