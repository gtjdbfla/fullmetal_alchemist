Outside Lansi’s northern gate, the road split in two. One branch went to the northern oasis, the other to the small villages to the west. Carts came and went along both without end.

“You’re going to look at all of this?” Ed asked.

“As much as I can,” Ling answered.

The first place they stopped was a small way station less than half a day from Lansi. It wasn’t big. One stable. Two inns. A workshop that repaired carts. A few warehouses.

But it was crowded. A caravan down from the north was changing horses, and merchants heading west were filling their water barrels. In front of the workshop, a young man was hammering the iron rim of a cart wheel.

“Have you worked here long?” Ling asked.

The man stopped his hammer. Once he realized the Emperor was standing in front of him, it took him a little while to answer. “…About twelve years.”

“Lots of people?”

“Depends on the season.”

“What if a new road opened in the south?”

The man’s eyes went to Luhan, then back to Ling. “You’re asking us?”

“Yeah.”

“I don’t know.”

Ling waited.

The man thought for a moment. “If there are fewer carts, there’s less work, I guess.”

“How much less?”

“I don’t know that either.”

“So you don’t want a new road?”

“Well…” The man scratched his head. “My younger brother lives in the south.”

“On the An side?”

“Yes. Down there, if you want to sell a single thing, you have to go the long way around for days.” He set down his hammer. “It’d be good for my brother.”

“And maybe bad for you?” Ed asked.

“Right.”

“So which is it?”

The man gave Ed a strange look. “It’s both.”

Ed shut his mouth.

Ling laughed. “He says it’s both.”

“I heard.”

“Your face is funny.”

“Shut up.”

They left the workshop. Luhan kept looking back for a while.

“Is he Bai?” Ling asked.

“No,” Luhan answered.

“But he’s one of the people you were worried about.”

“Yes.”

“Even though he’s not Bai?”

Luhan looked at Ling. “Just because I want to protect the Bai, should I look only at the Bai?”

Ling was quiet for a moment. “No.”

“Then that’s settled.”

Walking between them, Ed muttered, “You two really are alike.”

“How?” they both asked at the same time.

“That.”

Around noon, they went a little farther north. This time it was a bigger relay village. The marks of many caravans were posted on the warehouse walls, and some places run by Bai merchants stood out.

An old man who recognized Luhan came over. “Lord Luhan.”

“It’s been a while.”

“I heard you’d come to Lansi.”

The old man spotted Ling and hurriedly bowed. “Your Majesty.”

Ling waved a hand. “Just keep talking about whatever you were talking about.”

The old man looked at Luhan, troubled. “Is it about the southern road?”

“It is,” Luhan said. “Are you worried?”

“Of course I’m worried.”

“About the road itself?”

The old man shook his head. “People over there have to make a living too, don’t they?”

Ed looked at Luhan. Luhan kept listening without any reaction.

“It’s just that this place has lived with its eyes on this road alone,” the old man said. He gestured around them. “My son works at the warehouse, and my daughter-in-law works at the inn. My grandson is learning to tend horses.”

“A southern road won’t make this road disappear,” Ling said.

“I know.” The old man smiled. “And Your Majesty didn’t say you’d do away with the road here.”

“Then?”

“There’ll be fewer people.” The answer was simple. “If it’s a few fewer, it’ll be fine. If it’s a lot fewer, someone will have to find other work.”

“Does that frighten you?” Luhan asked.

The old man thought for a long time, then nodded. “Yes.” And he added, “But we can’t tell the people over there to keep living the hard way either.”

Ed kept his mouth shut. Both again. If you asked whether they were for it or against it, it was hard to answer. If you asked what they were afraid of, the answer came right away.

Ling seemed to be thinking the same thing. “Luhan.”

“Yes.”

“This is what you were talking about yesterday, isn’t it?”

“It is.”

“Then should we block the southern road because of these people?”

Luhan looked at the old man. The old man was waiting for the answer too.

“No,” Luhan said. “That would be like giving these people someone else’s opportunity.”

“Then what do we do?”

“Consider the southern road, but look at the people in the north who’ll lose out at the same time.”

“Like support?”

“If it’s needed.”

“And who decides that?”

Luhan paused for a moment. Then he said, “Your Majesty.”

Ling smiled a little. “It always comes back to that in the end.”

“Is that wrong?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Luhan’s eyebrow moved, ever so slightly.

Ed saw that expression. It had been the same yesterday. Every time Ling said he didn’t know, Luhan made a strange face for just a moment. Not so much disappointment as the face of someone looking at something unfamiliar.

On the way back, Ed finally asked. “You said you wanted to be emperor too.”

Luhan looked at him. “Yes.”

“Weren’t you disappointed when Ling became emperor?”

“Ed,” Ling cut in.

“What? I’m curious.”

“You’re asking that right in front of the person?”

“He’s right here. That’s why I’m asking him.”

“It’s all right,” Luhan said.

Ling closed his mouth.

Luhan walked for a moment looking straight ahead. “I was disappointed.”

Ed looked at Ling. Ling looked a little surprised too. “You were?”

“Isn’t that obvious?” Luhan said. “I grew up looking at nothing but that, ever since I was a child.”

“Becoming emperor?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

This time Luhan gave Ed a strange look. “To protect the Bai.”

It was an answer that seemed far too obvious to him. The smile left Ling’s face.

Luhan went on. “In my family, they always said the same thing. Clans close to the emperor survive. Clans that drift away from the emperor grow weak. So the emperor has to come from our clan.”

Ed looked at Ling. “You too?”

“Something like that,” Ling answered. “I thought that if I wanted to protect the Yao, I had to become emperor.”

“Thought?”

“I still have to protect the Yao,” Ling said. “But I’m not sure that’s necessarily the same thing as me being emperor.”

Luhan heard that and didn’t argue.

“So when you lost to Ling?” Ed asked.

“I thought it was over.”

“What was?”

“Becoming emperor.”

“Just like that?”

For the first time, Luhan looked a little offended. “It wasn’t easy.”

“But you said you congratulated him at the coronation.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Luhan thought for a moment. “The reason I tried to become emperor was to protect the Bai.”

“And?”

“Not becoming emperor doesn’t make the Bai disappear.”

Ling looked at him quietly.

“The only thing that changed was what I had to do,” Luhan said.

Ed slowed his steps a little. “So you’re still protecting the Bai?”

“Yes.”

“Without being emperor?”

“Is that strange?”

“No.” Ed shook his head. “If anything, that seems more normal.”

Ling laughed. “Hey, then what about me?”

“You’re the Emperor.”

“That’s your answer?”

“Yep.”

“That’s rude!”

Luhan laughed quietly. There didn’t seem to be any lingering regret in that laugh. At least not to Ed.

By the time they got back to Lansi, the sun had nearly set.

An old man they didn’t know was waiting in front of the government office. He looked close to sixty. He was dressed in the Bai style, but not showily.

When Luhan saw him, he stopped. “Zhenha.”

The old man bowed. “Lord Luhan.” Then he paid his respects to Ling as well. “Your Majesty.”

Ling looked at him for a moment. “Zhenha Bai?”

“You remember me.”

“I’ve seen you a few times before.”

Zhenha smiled. Then he looked at Luhan again. “I’ve been waiting a long time.”

“What is it?”

“The elders of the clan have something to tell you.”

Luhan looked at Ling. “I’ll go ahead first.”

“Sure.”

Before he turned away, Zhenha bowed his head to Ling. “I’ve heard Your Majesty will protect the land of the An.”

“…”

“I would be glad if you could say the same about the roads of the Bai.”

Ling was silent for a moment. “I’m listening.”

Luhan and Zhenha left.

Ed watched the two of them walk away. “Who’s that?”

“Someone who’s handled Bai politics for a long time. The caravan ledgers pass through his hands too,” Ling answered. “He’s been at Luhan’s side since Luhan was little.”

“So like a teacher?”

“More like…” Ling thought for a moment. “Someone who kept teaching Luhan why he had to become emperor.”

Ed looked at Zhenha’s back again. “He still needs someone like that?”

Ling didn’t answer.

Zhenha was simply walking beside Luhan. He wasn’t saying anything in particular. At least not here.

Evening.

Ed was going through old documents Lien had brought. He had asked for some old Lansi texts to compare with the records from Karsa. Most of them were nothing special. In such-and-such a year, such-and-such a caravan came. Such-and-such a herb was expensive. Such-and-such a well dried up. Records like that.

“…When am I supposed to read all this?”

Ed turned a page. Then his hand stopped.

An old travel record. Dated about four hundred years ago. A short record about a man from the west. No name.

Ed leaned forward.

The man from the west headed for the imperial palace. He spoke of the flow of the earth. He talked at length with those who practiced medicine.

So far, it wasn’t very different from the other records.

The next line. Ed’s eyes narrowed.

It said the emperor had tried to keep him at the palace. That he had wanted a man with such knowledge and skill close at hand. But the man had refused.

Ed read that part again. The record preserved his answer, briefly.

I learned from people, so I want to give it back to people.

Ed didn’t move for a while.

That sounds like Hohenheim.

That was the first thought. Then he frowned. “…I still don’t know.”

He was talking to himself. One more record didn’t make it certain they were the same man.

But it bothered him in a strange way. I learned from people. I give it back to people.

If it really had been Hohenheim—then maybe he wasn’t someone who had carried knowledge from west to east. Maybe he was someone who had kept learning the whole way. In Ishval. In Karsa. And in Xing.

Ed opened to the sentence he had written down in Karsa.

Being similar doesn’t mean having the same origin.

Below it, he wrote a new sentence.

Not who made it first, but what changed when they met.

His pen stopped.

Through the window, he could see the lights of Lansi. Beyond those lights was the northern road. And the southern road that didn’t exist yet.

Roads were made as people passed along them. People erased them too. Other people could make them again. But—

Whose did the road become?

Ed looked again at the sentence he had just read. I learned from people, so I want to give it back to people.

“…Give it back,” he murmured.

To whom?

There was a sound outside the door. It was Ling, with another bundle of papers in his hands.

“That again?” Ed asked.

“What?”

“Land and wells.”

“Yeah.”

“Is it okay for an emperor to read paperwork every night?”

“I’m reading it because I’m the Emperor.”

“Yesterday you said maybe an emperor shouldn’t decide everything.”

Ling stopped at the door. “That’s why I’m reading it.”

“Reading what?”

Ling looked down at the papers for a moment. “Who’s been deciding up to now.”

Ed didn’t answer.

This time, Ling looked at Ed’s desk. “And you?”

“The Sage of the West.”

“Hohenheim?”

“I told you, I don’t know yet.”

“Okay, okay.”

Ling started to leave.

“Ling,” Ed called.

“What?”

“You said you can protect your clan without being emperor.”

Ling stopped. “You mean what Luhan said?”

“Yeah.”

“Looks like it.”

“Then why are you still emperor?”

Ling looked at Ed. A pause.

“…I’m trying to figure that out too.”

Ling disappeared down the hallway.

Ed looked at the door, then lowered his eyes to the record again. The man from the west hadn’t stayed at the emperor’s side. Luhan was protecting the Bai even though he hadn’t become emperor. And Ling—only after becoming emperor had he started asking what an emperor was supposed to do.

Ed picked up his pen. He wrote a line, didn’t like it, and crossed it out. He wrote again.

Do the people with knowledge decide the answer?

A moment later, he added below it:

What about the people with power?

This time he didn’t cross it out.
