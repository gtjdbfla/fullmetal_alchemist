Seven in the morning.

Graus walked the planned survey site himself. Miles unfolded a map beside him.

“The first survey zone runs to here.”

“Smaller than I expected.”

“We only included areas agreed on with the residents.”

Red dots were scattered across the map, one added each time the reconstruction work turned something up. Several of them were circled in black.

Graus pointed. “This one.”

“An excluded zone.”

“Reason?”

“An old prayer site.”

The next. “A cemetery.”

And the next. “This is a prayer site too.”

Graus looked down at the map. “If all of that is excluded, can the strata be properly confirmed?”

“I don’t know.”

“You excluded them without knowing?”

“That is why we agreed to it.”

Graus looked at Miles. “The Fuhrer’s instructions?”

“At first.” Miles folded the map. “Now it is also my judgment.”

Graus was silent for a moment. “If the survey causes a clash, that would be putting the cart before the horse.”

“You agree?”

“A soldier does not need to agree with an agreement.” Graus started walking again. “Once it is settled, you keep it.”

People had already gathered at the first survey point. Cloth was spread beside a long trench in the ground, and a few dark red stones lay on it. Two geological engineers from Central were examining the samples. Adin was there with them.

“Is it the same stone?”

“The probability is high,” one engineer said. “But we can’t say for certain until the proper equipment arrives.”

“And the deposits?”

“That takes longer.”

Disappointment showed on Adin’s face.

“So drilling will be needed in the end?” Graus asked.

“Yes.” The engineer opened a map. “Here first, and if possible, this point would be best.”

“Not there,” Miles said at once.

“Why not?”

“It is inside the prayer site boundary.”

The engineer looked troubled. “But that’s the best place to confirm which way the strata run.”

“Find another place.”

Graus looked past a low stone wall at an old underground entrance. It was one of the places where the stone had first been found.

“Was the same stone found in there?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“It wasn’t dug out, so we don’t know.”

“And a survey would tell us?”

“It would be far more accurate.”

Graus looked at the entrance for a moment. “Then we will leave it unknown.”

The engineer looked at him. “Brigadier General?”

“It is an excluded zone, is it not?”

“But Central wants the potential deposits assessed as accurately as possible—”

“As accurately as possible. Not by any means necessary,” Graus said. “Survey within the conditions that have been set.”

Miles said nothing. He only looked at Graus once.

Around noon, one of the survey laborers drove in a stake to mark a boundary. A young Ishvalan nearby spotted it.

“Pull it out.”

The laborer turned. “What?”

“We told you not there.”

“I was told to mark it here.”

The young man grabbed the stake. “This is inside the prayer site.”

A soldier nearby came over. “What’s going on?”

“This man drove a stake inside the line.”

The soldier took out a map. “This is outside the boundary.”

“No, it isn’t.”

“According to the map—”

The young man yanked the stake out. On reflex, the soldier grabbed his wrist. “Hold on.”

The young man shook off his hand. “Don’t touch me.”

The people around them stopped.

The soldier’s face hardened. “Did you just shove a soldier?”

“You grabbed me first.”

People began to gather, one and then another.

“Here we go again.”

The soldier turned. “What did you say?”

“You said you wouldn’t dig up the land carelessly, and in the end you come in anyway.”

“We worked from the map.”

“And who made that map?”

Voices rose. The soldier’s hand moved toward his rifle. The muzzle was still pointed at the ground. But everyone’s eyes went to that hand at once.

Graus was watching the scene from a little way off. Fifteen or so people. One soldier. One stake. One gun.

In that moment, another scene overlapped it.

There was sand. There was blood.

“Lieutenant!”

Someone was calling him. Graus, then a young lieutenant, couldn’t answer.

It was his first real battle. There had been countless procedures taught at the military academy. But the moment the first soldier fell in front of him, the lines on the map lost all meaning.

“Lieutenant! Orders!”

Move left? Hold? Wait for reinforcements?

A few seconds. In those few seconds, another man fell.

“Lieutenant!”

“Move left!”

He gave the order. And regretted it at once.

“No, hold position!”

A second order. The soldiers stopped. In that moment, gunfire burst out. Another one went down.

Graus still remembered that soldier’s face. His name—

He couldn’t remember it. That was worse.

“Take your hand off the gun.”

The Ishval of the present came back.

The young soldier looked at Graus. “Brigadier General.”

“Your hand.”

The soldier took his hand off his rifle.

Graus looked at the young Ishvalan. “No one needs to die over a single stake.”

It went quiet around them.

“Miles.”

“Sir.”

“Check the boundary again.”

The military’s reconstruction map and the Ishvalan map of the old prayer sites. The two lines were different. On one, the stake was outside. On the other, it was inside.

Graus asked the laborer, “Who gave the instruction?”

“I followed the map I was given.”

“Good.” He looked at the young man. “And you.”

“On our map, it’s inside.”

“Confirmed.” Last of all, he looked at the soldier. “Why did you grab his wrist?”

“He pulled the stake out without authorization, so I tried to stop him.”

“Why did you reach for your gun?”

The soldier couldn’t answer. “People were crowding in, so…”

Graus looked around. “Were you attacked?”

“No, sir.”

“Did you see a weapon?”

“No, sir.”

“Then next time, do not reach for your gun first.”

The soldier lowered his head. “Yes, sir.”

“That is all the surveying for today,” Graus said.

The engineer was startled. “Brigadier General. If we just correct the boundary, we can start right—”

“We settle the boundary again first.”

“That’ll put us a day behind.”

Graus looked at the engineer. “Which costs more, a day or a corpse?”

The engineer shut his mouth.

After the people dispersed, Miles said, “That was quick.”

“What was?”

“Your judgment.”

Graus’s steps slowed for a moment. “There is no need to think long over something like that.”

The soldier from earlier was still standing alone. His hands were trembling a little.

“That soldier.”

“Sir.”

“Does he have combat experience?”

“I will check.”

“No,” Graus said. “Never mind.”

“Why?”

“I can tell from his face.”

He had been afraid. So he had reached for his gun.

Graus knew that feeling. Only the first thing he had grabbed for hadn’t been a gun. It had been an order.

1908.

Part of the front had collapsed. Graus’s platoon had lost nearly half its men. The order for support was late. Contact with the neighboring unit had been cut. No one knew who was supposed to move where.

And then the senior command arrived.

It was the first time Graus had seen that man up close.

King Bradley.

The Fuhrer looked at the wounded. He asked where the line had broken. He confirmed how many troops were left. He confirmed the position of the reinforcements.

That was all.

“Pull Second Platoon back.”

“Sir!”

“Third Platoon holds this line.” His finger moved across the map. “The reserve swings around this way.”

Someone asked, “Enemy numbers are larger than expected.”

Bradley didn’t even raise his head. “And?”

“Waiting for reinforcements might—”

“And give up this line in the meantime?”

No one could answer.

The orders came down. People moved. The front that had been in a tangle moments before took shape again.

Graus watched in a daze. What he hadn’t been able to decide for several minutes, that man had finished in a few seconds.

And on another front, a little way off, there was one more man. Major Roy Mustang. State Alchemist. Only a year older than Graus.

Flames flared in the distance. Once. Twice. The shape of the battlefield changed.

A battlefield Graus hadn’t been able to move had moved at the snap of that man’s fingers.

After the battle, Bradley called for him. “Are you the commander here?”

Graus straightened. “I am, sir.”

There was blood on his uniform. He didn’t know whether it was his own or his men’s.

“Your first battle?”

“…Yes, sir.”

Bradley looked once at the dead soldiers. “How many did you lose?”

Graus answered. The moment the number left his mouth, it felt strangely light. As if they had become numbers instead of people.

“Remember this,” Bradley said.

Graus looked up.

“A commander’s hesitation is paid for by his men.”

That was all. But Graus did not forget those words for nine years.

“Brigadier General?”

At Miles’s voice, Graus came back to reality. “What?”

“You seemed far away for a moment.”

Graus walked on. “Rotate the guards.”

“Disciplinary action?”

“No.”

“Then?”

“Retrain them first,” Graus said. “Remind them this is not a battlefield.”

Miles nodded. “Understood.”

A few steps later, Graus added, “And designate a standby unit.”

Miles stopped. “A standby unit?”

“Do not deploy it to the site.”

“May I ask why?”

“What do you think would have happened today if that soldier had raised his gun?”

“He did not raise it.”

“I know.”

“Then why?”

“Being prepared and being sent in are different matters.”

Miles looked at him for a moment. “Understood.”

Graus called to a young second lieutenant behind them. “Second Lieutenant Bark.”

“Yes, Brigadier General.”

“You will handle the guards’ retraining. If residents are unarmed, no one touches a gun first.”

“Understood.”

“If residents attack first, they are to be subdued. Not shot.”

“Understood.”

“Questions?”

“I would only like to confirm the standard for subduing.”

“Go on.”

“Until they are no longer capable of a further attack?”

Graus’s gaze paused for a moment. “That does not mean kill them.”

“I understand.”

“Good.”

Bark saluted and turned away. He had asked one question. He had needed one answer. As soldiers went, that wasn’t bad.

Of the twelve men Graus had brought as escorts for the investigation, he was the second lieutenant who stood out the most. In this land, those twelve were the only troops Graus could command directly.

Late in the afternoon, when Graus returned to his temporary office, there was a document on his desk.

Guidelines on the Ishval Crimstone Survey and the Maintenance of Public Order.

It had come down from the Fuhrer’s office several weeks earlier.

No drilling for mining purposes shall be carried out without residents’ consent.

The military shall not independently designate mining zones.

In the event of disputes involving land or religious sites, consultation with residents shall take priority.

Graus turned to the last page. Approved by: Roy Mustang.

His hand stopped. It wasn’t very different from the judgment he himself had made today. If anything, it was reasonable.

Graus looked down at the signature for a while. Then he said, very quietly, “…Hypocrite.”

He closed the document.

There was a knock at the door.

“Come in.”

It was Bark. “I have completed the guards’ retraining.”

Graus looked at the clock. “Already?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Problems?”

“None.”

“Apply it starting tomorrow.”

“Understood.”

Bark started to turn, then stopped. “I have an additional report.”

“Go on.”

“I have completed the terrain survey of the northeastern survey zone.”

Graus looked up. It was the zone he had ordered withdrawn from in the afternoon, when the sandstorm grew strong.

“Did you not hear the order to withdraw?”

“I heard it.”

“And?”

“I returned after completing the survey of my assigned zone.”

Graus’s face hardened. “If you are told to withdraw, you withdraw.”

“Yes, sir.”

“It could have waited until tomorrow.”

Bark didn’t answer.

“Why did you stay?”

“I had already received an instruction to finish by today.”

Graus looked at him. “Was it not dangerous?”

“It was dangerous.”

“And?”

“I was still able to complete it.”

There was no pride in it, and no excuse. It was simply a voice reporting a result.

“From now on, when an order to withdraw comes down, give it priority.”

“Understood.”

“Dismissed.”

“Sir.”

The door closed.

Graus looked down at Bark’s report for a moment. Not a single item was missing. The map markings were precise. By regulation, this was something he should have reprimanded. But the result was perfect.

Next to Bark’s name, Graus made a short note.

Finishes whatever he is given.

At the time, that looked like a strength.

At dusk, Scar met Zahir where the stake had been pulled up.

“Today it was a single stake,” Zahir said. “Next it will be a hole. Then a road. Then a mine.”

“That has not happened yet.”

“A soldier laid hands on one of our people.”

“Another soldier stopped him,” Scar said.

“That is nothing to be grateful for. It is how it should have been to begin with.”

Scar looked at the empty hole. “Then we will see whether it is the same next time.”

That night, Graus wrote an additional report.

Dispute caused by an error in survey boundary markings. Approx. 15 residents gathered. No armed action. No basis for organized activity. Direct cause: discrepancy between military map and prayer site boundary records. Survey suspended; crowd dispersed voluntarily.

If he had left only “residents gathered” and “friction with the military,” it would have been a different report. Graus signed it without taking any facts out.

Beside it lay the early public order records from Ishval in 1901.

Friction during inspection at a checkpoint.

Protesters gather.

Residents join.

Weapons confirmed.

Gunfire.

A few pages later, the words began to change. From public order to unrest. To clashes. To combat. To war.

Graus went back to the first page. Today fifteen people had gathered, but there had been no gunfire and no one hurt. That was how the record would read.

He set the two records side by side. Before one line turned into the next, what had people seen that made them afraid?

Outside the window, Ishval was quiet.
