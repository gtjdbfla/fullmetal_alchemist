Scar was reading his brother’s records. Ed was reading the record of the man with no name.

It wasn’t even something the traveler had written himself. It was a record that had passed through many hands. Ed was reading the same sentence for the third time.

He took what he had learned and headed east.

“Staring at it will not make more words appear,” Scar said beside him.

“I know.”

“Did you not read the same record yesterday? What more are you looking for?”

Ed put the record down. “What’s missing.”

“What is missing?”

“There’s no name and no date. Just that he came from the west.”

“He lived several hundred years ago.”

“That’s why I have to look harder.”

“Why?”

“Because I want to know.”

Just then the door opened, and Sahara came in. “You’re both up early.”

“Any older records?” Ed asked at once.

“Shouldn’t ‘good morning’ come first?”

“Good morning. Any older records?”

Instead of the records, Sahara looked at Scar. “Today, let’s start with your brother.”

Scar stood up. “What do you mean to show me?”

“A person.”

“A person?”

“Someone who remembers your brother.”

Scar’s expression changed.

The place Sahara took them was a field at the southern edge of Karsa. A man was repairing a collapsed stone wall.

“Majid,” Sahara called.

The man raised his head. His eyes rested on Scar’s face. “…Idris’s brother?”

Scar took a step closer. “You remember my brother?”

“I do.” Majid looked Scar over once more. “You don’t look much like him.”

His gaze dropped to the lines carved into Scar’s right arm. Majid snorted. “You drew a lot on your arm too, like your brother?”

Scar looked down at his arm.

“I drew the first few lines on your brother’s arm,” Majid said. “He said he wanted to try Karsa’s method with his own body. Even though I told him we don’t draw on our bodies.”

“And the result?”

“He was sick for two days, and then he laughed. Said something had reached him.”

Scar smiled, very briefly. It was a bitter smile. Ed saw his face but said nothing.

Majid’s hand stopped as he lifted a stone into place. “…Where is he living now? Is he well?”

“He is dead.”

It was a short answer.

Majid was silent for a while. “…I see.”

He went back to fixing the wall as he spoke. “He was a man with a lot of questions. At first he only asked why the stone and the water moved.”

Ed looked at Scar. Scar nodded to Majid to go on.

“But after a few days, what he asked changed.”

Scar’s gaze came back to him. “How?”

“He asked whether the water could be sent farther.” Majid ran his finger along the channel. “Whether it could be done where there was little rain. What to do in land where the wells kept drying up. What to do when the ground collapsed in sandy places.” His hand stopped. “And he kept talking about Ishval.”

Scar’s expression stiffened. “What about it?”

“That there wasn’t enough water. That people lived far apart from each other. That there were still people who had to live there.” Majid looked at Scar. “He asked whether what we do here could be done in Ishval too.”

Scar’s hand went still. “My brother meant to take this home?”

“At first, I thought so too.”

“At first?”

Majid shook his head. “Later, no.”

“Why?”

“He said the land was different.”

Ed reacted.

Majid went on. “The water is different. The wind is different. The way people have lived is different. He said you couldn’t just take what works in Karsa and carry it over as it is.”

Ed’s eyes narrowed.

“Then what did he mean to take?” Scar asked.

Majid thought for a moment. “A way of seeing.”

“…A way of seeing?”

“That’s how he put it.” Majid pointed at the ground. “If you can’t make water, first see where the water you have is going. If you can’t change the land, first see where it’s crumbling. Then look for what you can do.”

Scar said nothing.

Quietly, Ed unfolded the page of his brother’s record that Scar had copied. On it was a sentence that a few days ago had been just one record among many.

In places with little water, before thinking about how to make water, think first about how not to lose the water you have.

Ed handed the paper back to Scar. Scar looked at the sentence for a long time.

“…So that was why.”

“What was?” Majid asked.

“My brother’s records.” Scar’s voice was low. “I did not know why he had written such things.”

The width of the channels. The slope of the land. Terrain suited to digging wells. Ways of storing water. One by one, the things in his brother’s records began to take on a different meaning. His brother hadn’t come east only to gather knowledge.

“What did my brother say when he left?” Scar asked.

“That he was going east.”

“East.”

“That he’d go all the way to Xing to learn more about alkahestry.” Majid added, “And that once he had learned it, he’d go back to Ishval.”

Scar’s hand slowly closed into a fist. “…I see.”

That was all. Sahara said nothing either.

After a moment, Ed asked, “You okay?”

Scar looked at him. “About what?”

“Nothing.” Ed scratched his head. “Just, it seems like you found out what you wanted to know.”

Scar lowered his head without a word. “I know a little of what he saw.”

“Yeah.”

“But why he left it behind…” A pause. “I do not know all of that yet.”

Ed snorted. “You use that word too.”

“What word?”

“’Yet.’”

Scar didn’t answer. Instead he asked, “Is it not your turn now?”

“My turn?”

“The man from the west.”

Ed’s expression changed instantly. “Right!”

“You are loud.”

“Majid, do you know about him too?”

“How would I know someone from several hundred years ago?”

“…Fair.”

Sahara laughed. “We found one more record.”

Ed spun around at once. “Why are you only telling me now?!”

“I wanted to see whether you could listen to someone else’s story.”

“I listened!”

“Barely.”

Nadir had gotten to the record room first. A few worn ledgers lay on the table. Daud was copying out records off to one side.

When Ed came in, Daud said, “You again.”

“You mind?”

“It’s fine if you’re quiet.”

“That’ll be hard.”

“I know.”

Nadir pushed a ledger toward Ed. “Let me say this first.”

“Say what?”

“We can’t confirm that these records all point to the same person.”

Ed nodded. “But the dates and contents connect, right?”

“Yes.”

“Then that’s fine.”

“It is not fine.” Nadir looked Ed straight in the eye. “It means it’s not certain.”

Ed looked at him for a moment. Then he snorted. “I know.”

Daud looked surprised. “You’re admitting it without a fight.”

“I’m growing too, you know.”

“You have grown a great deal in a few days,” Scar said from behind him.

“You’re not talking about height, right?”

“No one said anything about height.”

“Then fine.”

Ed opened the first record. A traveler from the west. Stayed in Karsa and spent a long time studying the waterways and the structure of the stone. Asked how far the movement of the earth extended. He already knew that.

The second record. Left for the east. He knew that too.

“Anything new?”

Nadir pointed at the third ledger. “Here.”

Ed leaned in close. It was a part that recorded the traveler’s appearance. A tall man. Western clothes. An unfamiliar way of speaking. And—

Ed’s hand stopped.

Golden hair.

“…”

Scar came over beside him. “What?”

“Hang on.”

Ed read the next line. Part of it had blurred. But one word describing the eyes remained.

Golden.

Ed’s face went rigid. “When is this record from?”

“There’s no exact date,” Nadir said.

“Even roughly.”

Sahara opened another record. “That’s what took us so long to find.”

It was a record compiled by later generations about events in Karsa and the villagers of that time. Sahara pointed to one part. “If you read it alongside these records, you can narrow it down.”

“By how much?”

“It’s not a matter of a few generations.”

Ed looked up.

“About four hundred years ago,” Sahara said.

Ed froze.

Scar watched Ed’s face. “Is it an era you know?”

“…Wait.”

Ed read the record again. Golden hair. Golden eyes. Four hundred years ago. Came from the west. Studied Karsa’s flow. And then went east.

Something in his head began to connect. But Ed kept his mouth shut. “Where did he come from?”

Nadir took out the last ledger. “This one is the most uncertain.”

“Why?”

“It isn’t an original. It’s an old record that someone copied later.”

“Show me.”

Ed took it. A few lines couldn’t be read at all. A man who had crossed the western desert. Came from farther west. And a faint place name.

Ed’s finger stopped. “…Xerxes.”

“Xerxes?” Scar repeated.

Ed didn’t answer. This time his memories connected too quickly. Four hundred years ago. Xerxes. Golden hair. Golden eyes. A man who left for the east. And—

The Sage of the West.

Ed put the ledger down. A face rose in his mind. A man who rarely talked about himself. A man who had lived for hundreds of years and wandered Amestris. Who had passed through too many places. Who had known too many things.

The words slipped out of Ed’s mouth. “…Dad?”

The room went quiet.

Daud looked at Ed. “Your father lived four hundred years ago?”

“It’s a long story.”

“Must be a very long one.”

“Really long.”

“Are you certain?” Scar asked.

Ed looked down at the record. Before, he might have already settled on an answer. But he remembered how many times he had been wrong over the past few days. He had thought Karsa’s method was the beginning of alkahestry. It wasn’t. He had thought the traveler from the west might have taken Karsa’s knowledge east and created alkahestry. That wasn’t so simple either.

Ed shook his head. “No.”

Scar narrowed his eyes. “No?”

“It’s not confirmed yet.” Ed touched the records one by one. “Golden hair. Golden eyes. Four hundred years ago. Xerxes. And he went east.” He paused. “A lot of it does fit.”

“Then is it not nearly certain?” Scar said.

“Nearly and certain are different things.”

Nadir looked at Ed. “You’re better than yesterday.”

“At what?”

“Not deciding the answer first.”

Ed scowled. “Why does everyone here have to put in a word?”

“Because your reactions are fun,” Daud said.

“Get out here.”

“No.”

Sahara opened one more ledger. “There’s an interesting record about that traveler.”

Ed leaned in immediately.

At first it was much like what they had already read. Why does stone move? Where does the movement of water begin? Does the earth have a flow too?

“…It’s the same,” Ed muttered.

“As you?” Scar asked.

“Yeah.”

The next record. The traveler had spent a long time watching the people of Karsa build walls and channels. And he had also explained knowledge from the west that he knew. Some of it the people of Karsa took in. Some of it they didn’t use.

Ed turned the page. “He didn’t only teach.”

“Of course not,” Sahara said.

“He learned too.”

“That’s usually what happens when people meet.”

Ed read the last sentence.

Said he wished to know whether what he had learned here could be seen in other lands as well.

Ed’s eyes stopped. “In other lands…”

“What is it?” Scar asked.

Ed didn’t answer. He went back over the records from the beginning. Karsa’s method had existed before this traveler came. In the east, people had talked about the flow of the earth and of energy since before that too. Then even if this man was Hohenheim—

Hohenheim wasn’t the beginning of everything. He too had met things that already existed. He had learned. He had left behind what he knew. And then he had moved on.

“If it really is Dad…” Ed muttered.

Scar looked at him. “What?”

“He didn’t come here to give something.”

“He gave things too, I’m sure,” Sahara said.

“I know.” Ed nodded. “But that’s not all.” He tapped the record. “He came to learn.”

He thought for a moment. “No. Maybe he was just wandering around and stumbled onto it.”

Sahara laughed. “That sounds more natural.”

“Either way, once he saw it, he couldn’t just walk past.”

“Like you,” Scar said.

Ed shut his mouth.

Daud laughed. “So there were people like that hundreds of years ago too.”

“Don’t call me ‘people like that’!”

But he couldn’t deny it. The moment Ed had first seen the walls of Karsa move, he had thrown himself at them. Scar’s brother had been the same. And so had this traveler. Different eras. Different purposes. But in the moment they first came face to face with it, they had been the same. They saw something they didn’t know. They stopped. And they asked.

Ed snorted. “Maybe it really is Dad.”

“Is that your reason?” Scar asked.

“What?”

“His temperament.”

“…I don’t like that.”

In the afternoon, Scar stood for a long time beside the stone wall Majid had been mending. He knew a little now of what his brother had seen, but he still didn’t know how far he had gone.

When he climbed to the top of the village, Ed was looking east. Houses, fields, and channels ran on between the cliffs.

“Will you go east?”

“Yeah. I need to check whether that traveler was Dad.”

“Is that all?”

“And what he learned here before he left.” Ed pointed at Karsa’s waterways. “This was here before Dad came, right? There’s probably knowledge in Xing that was there before him too.”

“You may be wrong again.”

“Then I’ll look again.”

Scar didn’t answer. Ed didn’t press him to come along either. Where Scar would go, carrying the question his brother had left behind, was for Scar to decide.

At the fork, the water in the channel split between the fields and the village below. Ed watched for a while, then took out his notebook.

Traveler from the west = Hohenheim?

He didn’t erase the question mark.

“Guess I’d better go see.”

“To find what?”

Ed smiled. “I don’t know. My questions have changed.”
