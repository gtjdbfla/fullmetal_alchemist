Central, Amestris.

The trial did not open right away.

After Graus was detained in Ishval, an investigation team was formed. Orders and communication records were gathered. Reports from Miles and Hawkeye came in, and the four soldiers who had moved with Bark were questioned one after another. Bark’s gun and the evidence recovered from the first three victims were sent for examination. The mortar used in the southern shelling was identified as an old model that had gone missing from the army’s disposal list, but who had taken it was never established.

The investigation continued even after Brandt’s funeral. The soldier who had shot Brandt was questioned with Ishvalan residents’ representatives observing. The commander of the battalion that had stood by at the terminus was summoned and questioned, and the soldiers testified that they had only been told it was a joint mobile exercise.

Several weeks passed that way.

Three days before the formal trial, Mustang visited the military detention facility in Central. Alone.

The door opened. Graus raised his head.

One desk. Two chairs. Iron bars set into the window.

“Your Excellency.”

“Sit.”

Graus sat. Mustang pulled out the chair across from him.

“Is this an interrogation?”

“No.”

“Then why have you come?”

“There’s something I want to know.” Mustang looked at him. “Why me?”

Graus didn’t answer.

“You made a war in Ishval. You meant to grow it until I came myself.”

“Yes.”

“Did you think it would end once I came?”

“The opposite.”

“What?”

“I thought Your Excellency would not be able to end it.”

Mustang’s eyes narrowed. “Why?”

“Because you could not kill Ishvalans again.”

The room went quiet.

“Your Excellency took part in the Ishvalan War of Extermination.”

“I know.”

“You killed many.”

Mustang’s expression hardened. “I know that too.”

“So I thought you would never be able to use the same method again. Negotiating, separating, waiting…”

“…while the war drags on.”

“And then you end it?”

“Yes.”

Mustang tapped the desk with his finger. Then stopped. “But here’s the thing.”

“Yes?”

“What if I’d just pushed through?”

Graus’s eyes moved.

“Against your prediction, sent in the troops,” Mustang said. “What if I’d ended the war even if it meant killing Ishvalans again?”

A moment of silence passed.

Graus said, “That would not have been bad either.”

The expression drained from Mustang’s face. “What?”

“Your Excellency has pursued the reconstruction of Ishval.” Graus’s voice was calm. “You said you would set right the wrongs of the past, and give the people of Ishval another chance.”

“…”

“In the first war in Ishval since such a man became Fuhrer…” Graus looked Mustang straight in the eye. “…he kills Ishvalans again.”

Mustang’s finger went rigid.

“Then what would everything you have said until now become?”

“…”

“Would the people of Ishval trust the Fuhrer again?” Graus said. “And the reformers who supported you? The people who believed you were different from Bradley?”

“So either way, it didn’t matter to you,” Mustang said quietly.

“It mattered.”

“Which way did you prefer?”

“The way without killing.”

“Why?”

“Because that way, I would be needed.”

Mustang’s eyes turned cold. “I can’t end the war because I can’t kill people.”

“Yes.”

“So you end it.”

“Yes.”

“But if I had killed…”

“Your Excellency would have won.” Graus paused for a moment. “The war.”

“And politically?”

“You would have lost a great deal.”

Mustang leaned back in his chair. “Nicely done.” There was no trace of a smile. “Don’t kill, and I’m incompetent.”

“…”

“Kill, and I’m a hypocrite.”

Graus didn’t deny it.

“Either way, I come out weaker.”

“That was my intention.”

“And you rise.”

“Yes.”

Mustang looked at him for a long time. “So you’d bring me down…” A beat. “…and become Fuhrer yourself?”

Graus looked at Mustang. And said, “Did you not do the same?”

“What?”

“Fuhrer King Bradley.” Graus’s voice was still calm. “Did you not move your own people to try to bring that man down?”

“That’s—”

“And in the end, you took that seat.”

Mustang closed his mouth.

“Is it different?”

“It’s different.” The answer was quick.

“I suppose it is.”

“Are you being sarcastic?”

“No,” Graus said. “I mean that Your Excellency would think so.”

Mustang’s eyebrow moved.

“I do not know what happened on the Promised Day.”

“…”

“Fuhrer Bradley died.”

“…”

“You rebelled against him and survived,” Graus said, one point at a time. “And in the end, you became Fuhrer.”

Mustang’s mouth opened. You don’t know what happened that day—

He meant to say it. He stopped.

Graus didn’t know. The homunculi. The dwarf in the flask. A transmutation circle that used the entire country. What they had actually stopped on the Promised Day. What Bradley had been.

It wasn’t only Graus who didn’t know. Most of this country didn’t. The history they could see was far simpler. There was Bradley. Mustang rebelled. Bradley died. And Mustang became Fuhrer.

Mustang slowly leaned back in his chair. “…I suppose it could look that way.”

This time it was Graus who looked a little surprised. “What?”

“The me you see,” Mustang said. “I’d never thought about it.”

Graus looked away first. “On the Promised Day, I was a lieutenant colonel.”

“I know.”

“I was not incompetent either.”

“I know that too.”

“And yet that day, I was nothing.”

Mustang’s eyebrow moved. “What do you mean?”

“The top of the army collapsed,” Graus said. “Fuhrer Bradley died, and many generals died or were pushed out of their posts.”

Mustang listened quietly.

“And other names remained.”

“…”

“Mustang.” A beat. “Armstrong.” Another beat. “Miles.”

Graus let out a short laugh. “Major General Olivier Mira Armstrong is a full general now.”

“She deserves it.”

“I know.” The answer was quick.

Mustang looked at him. “And that made it worse, knowing?”

Graus smiled briefly. “…There may have been some of that.”

“Miles too?”

“He became a colonel. Colonel Miles is capable as well.”

“Then what’s the problem?”

Graus didn’t answer for a long while. “What if I had been there that day?” For the first time, a small crack appeared in his voice. “I thought about it sometimes.”

Mustang said nothing.

“I was not there that day.”

“And?”

“And so afterward, I kept doing what I had always done,” Graus said. “I went from battlefield to battlefield. I took on operations. I produced results.”

“And became a brigadier general.”

“Yes.” Graus looked straight at Mustang. “I was promoted too.”

“Want me to congratulate you?”

“No.” The corner of Graus’s mouth moved very slightly, then was gone. “I was promoted.” He was silent for a moment. “And those people became history.”

Mustang couldn’t fire back right away.

“You were always in the middle of it.”

“You think I wanted that?”

“At least I did,” Graus said. “I wanted to be in the middle of it.”

Mustang looked at him for a long time. “Were you jealous of me, or something?”

Graus laughed shortly. “If I said no, would you believe me?”

“No.”

“Then let us say I was.”

“Is that all?”

The laugh disappeared. “No.” The answer was fast. “If that were all, I would not have gone so far as to use Ishval.”

“I respected Fuhrer Bradley.”

“Why?”

“Because he decided.”

“Decided what?”

“What had to be done.”

Mustang’s expression turned cold. “Killing the people of Ishval?”

“I am not saying I liked the result.”

“Convenient thing to say.”

“Perhaps it is.” Graus didn’t look away. “But the war ended.”

“Kill everyone and the war ends.”

“Yes.”

It was far too fast an answer. Mustang’s eyes narrowed.

“So I thought Your Excellency would lose either way.”

“And?”

Graus was silent for a moment. “I was wrong.”

“About what?”

“I thought you would choose one or the other,” Graus said. “Your Excellency did not kill.”

“…”

“And yet you ended it.” A beat. “That was not in my calculations.”

Mustang said nothing for a while. “The battalion at the terminus.”

Graus’s hand moved very slightly.

“If I’d failed, those troops would have come in.”

“Within three days they would have been standing outside Ishval.”

“To end the war.”

“Yes.”

“Without the Fuhrer’s orders.”

“In the place where Your Excellency had failed to end it.”

“Even though I ordered them stopped at the terminus.”

“That battalion knows my name first.”

Mustang didn’t answer for a long while. “They may know your name…”

“…”

“…but they don’t know their country.”

Graus couldn’t answer.

“If those troops had come in and killed people, who would the hypocrite have been?” Mustang said quietly.

Graus kept his mouth shut for a long time, then said, “…It would have been me.”

“And Bark?”

“I chose him.”

“When the first civilian died, did you know?”

“Yes.”

“But you didn’t stop it. Not with the second either.”

“Yes.”

“’Avoid them if possible.’ It wasn’t a ban.”

“…Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it was not enough. The war.”

The room went quiet.

“The third was a child.”

“Yes.”

“Only then did you stop him. But you kept the operation going.”

“Because you had not come yet.”

Mustang looked at him for a long time. “Did you think you could control it?”

“Yes.”

“Bark?”

“Yes.”

“The war too?”

The answer came back a little late. “Yes.”

“In the end, you couldn’t control either.”

Graus didn’t deny it.

“About the soldier who shot Councilman Brandt.”

Graus raised his head.

“He’s the soldier who reached for his gun during the stake incident. The one you told not to reach for his gun first.”

“…I remember.”

“Teaching alone wasn’t enough.”

Graus couldn’t answer.

“Do you remember the names of the first three?”

Graus didn’t answer.

“Hamid. Thirty-eight. He hauled lumber in the reconstruction zone.” Mustang read from the papers. “Layla. Forty-two. She sold cloth at the market.”

“…”

“Yunus. Eight years old.”

Graus’s hand trembled, very slightly. “…Hamid,” he repeated quietly. “Layla. Yunus.”

It was as if he were taking the names into his mouth one at a time.

“At the trial, you’ll be reading them out yourself,” Mustang said. “Memorize them.”

“I did not order the attack on Scar.”

“I know.”

“The withdrawal order was real too.”

“I know.”

“I do not want those parts recorded as my orders.”

Mustang nodded. “They won’t be.”

Graus looked at him. “Why?”

“Because what you did is enough on its own.”

Mustang stood up. He headed for the door, then stopped. “Graus.”

“Yes.”

“You asked me something earlier.”

“What was that?”

“Whether you and I are different.”

Graus closed his mouth.

“I suppose we are,” Mustang said. “You and me.”

“…”

“And Bradley and I are probably different too.”

“I suppose so.”

“But…”

He remembered Brandt lying on the ground. His last words too. Do not replace the person. Change the system.

“That doesn’t change the seat,” Mustang said.

Graus’s eyes moved.

“Bradley died.”

“…”

“I sat down in his place.” Mustang looked at the door. “If I get it wrong, the next soldier will pull me down and sit there.”

Graus didn’t answer.

“And the one after that.” Mustang let out a hollow laugh. “What a well-made country.”

“Then what will you do?”

Mustang didn’t answer right away. “You were right about one thing.”

“What was that?”

“That I brought Bradley down and sat in this seat too.”

“…”

“So that’s where the problem was.”

“What do you mean?” Graus asked.

Mustang opened the door. “Soldiers pulling down soldiers,” he said, “and sitting in that seat. A country like that.”

Captain Hawkeye was waiting in the corridor. “Is it over?”

“Yes.”

They walked side by side. For a while, neither of them spoke.

“Captain Hawkeye.”

“Sir.”

“Do you remember what Councilman Brandt said at the end?”

“I remember.”

“He told me not to replace the person.”

“Yes, sir.”

“To change the system.”

“Yes, sir.”

Mustang stopped walking. “I understand now.”

Hawkeye looked at him.

“Choosing the next Fuhrer well isn’t enough.”

“…”

“Whether the next one is better than me,” Mustang said. “Whether he’s better than Bradley.” He shook his head. “If it’s a country that has to keep checking that, it’s the same.”

“What do you intend to do?”

Mustang looked at the window at the end of the corridor. He could see Central. Military headquarters. Government offices. And the seat above all of them. The Fuhrer. Supreme commander of the military, and the highest power in the nation.

Mustang couldn’t answer right away. “I don’t know.”

Hawkeye looked at him.

“But this country has to change,” Mustang said. “Not who sits there, but the seat itself.” A beat. “I don’t have a way yet.”

Hawkeye was silent for a moment. “It seems there will be a lot of work.”

Mustang snorted. “Looks like it.”

“Do you regret it?”

“I haven’t even started yet.”

The corners of Hawkeye’s mouth moved very slightly. They walked on again.

Three days later, Graus’s trial began. What he had ordered. What he had known. When he could have stopped. And why he hadn’t. One by one, it began to be recorded.

But for Mustang, the trial had already left a different question behind.

Why had this country always left the answer to a single soldier?
