Ishval, Amestris.

The closer Ed got to Ishval, the stranger the stories he heard.

There had been a war. No, a riot. The army had attacked Ishval again. The Fuhrer had come in person. The Fuhrer had ended the war. Some said the Ishvalans had fired first, and some said the army had killed civilians. He even heard that some high-ranking person from Central had died in Ishval.

Everyone told it like fact, but none of it fit together. Only one thing was certain. While he was in Xing, something big had happened in Ishval.

When the buildings of Ishval came into view, Ed walked faster.

But it was strange. He couldn’t hear any gunfire.

Where checkpoints had stood, only torn-down fences and a few wooden stakes remained. On the road where military transports had gone back and forth, handcarts were passing. Among the ruined buildings, there was the sound of hammers again. People still looked at uniforms for a long time. And soldiers, when they went into the alleys, kept their hands well away from their hips.

He’d heard there had been a war. But now it didn’t look like one.

“What the…” Ed muttered. “What on earth happened here?”

“It is a long explanation.”

The voice came from beside him. Ed spun around. It was Scar.

“You!”

Scar was fine. Without meaning to, Ed looked him up and down.

“You’re alive.”

“You can see that.”

“I heard all kinds of stuff on the way here!” Ed strode closer. “That there was a war, that the army invaded again, that Mustang came in person—”

“Some of it is true.”

“That’s the worst possible answer!”

Scar turned. “Let us go to Colonel Miles.”

“Wait.” Ed followed him. “Was it really a war?”

Scar slowed down. “For a while.”

“Who started it?”

“It is not known.”

Ed frowned. “That the army attacked first?”

“It is not that simple.”

“That civilians died?”

Scar’s expression hardened. “That is true.”

The irritation left Ed’s face too. “…And Mustang?”

“He came.”

“And?”

“He did not kill anyone.”

Ed stopped short.

Scar didn’t look back. “He saved people with fire.”

“…What are you talking about?”

“Hear it from him yourself.”

“That’s all you’ve been saying!”

“I told you it was a long explanation.”

“So explain it!”

Scar ignored him. Ed ground his teeth and followed.

Then he looked around again. Walls pocked with bullet holes. Checkpoints being cleared away. Shops opening their doors again. The fighting seemed to be over. But it didn’t look like a place where nothing had happened.

When they walked into the temporary building of the reconstruction committee, Miles looked up from his papers. “You’re back.”

“Yeah.”

Before he’d even pulled out a chair, Ed asked, “What happened here?”

Miles set down the papers he was holding. “How much have you heard?”

“There was a war.” Ed held up one finger. “Mustang came.” Two. “Civilians died.” Three. “Some high-ranking guy from Central died here.” Four. He lowered his hand. “And everyone who tells it tells it differently.”

Miles thought for a moment. “Then let me start with what has been confirmed. There was a string of small incidents, and three civilians were shot, one after another. Who fired is now being dealt with at the trial. After that, rumors traveled faster than gunfire, and the Fuhrer came, reduced the checkpoints, and changed the supply routes.”

“Mustang did?”

“He stopped the shells with flame, too,” Scar said.

“…Seriously?”

“I saw it.”

Ed scratched his head. “Did anything happen to you?”

“There was a marksman aiming at me,” Scar said. “Captain Hawkeye found him first. She was the one who fired.”

“…She did?”

“If not for her, I would be dead.”

“And the councilman, Brandt?”

Miles’s face hardened. “He is dead. An Ishvalan pulled out a knife, and the councilman stepped in between. It was a soldier who fired. Whether that is the whole answer to what happened is still under investigation.”

“Under investigation,” Ed repeated. It was like what he’d seen in Lansi. “I saw that a few times in Xing too.”

Miles nodded. Then he took another document from one side of the desk. “You came at a good time.”

Ed got wary. “Why?”

“There is something I want to show you.”

The moment he saw the words on the cover, Ed’s eyes changed.

Survey Results: Dark Red Mineral Strata.

“There is no alchemically unusual reaction.”

“We knew that already.”

“It needed official confirmation.” Miles turned to the next page. “The problem is the quantity.”

Ed took the paper. Mineral strata confirmed during reconstruction. Estimated extent. Areas that could be mined.

“…That’s a lot.”

“Yes.”

In Amestris it was rare. It held up to heat and pressure, which made it valuable as a material for certain alloys. In Xing, it was treated as a gem. But in old Ishval, it had been different. Something that belonged to the land. Something you didn’t take home. Something you didn’t trade for money. Stone used in places of prayer.

“Has Central asked for it?”

“No official request yet.”

“Yet?”

“Once its value gets out, there will be talk.”

“Will the army take it?” Scar asked.

“That has not been decided either.”

Ed put the document down. In the old days, his next questions would already have been settled. Why was it concentrated here? Did it have to do with alchemy? What had the ancient Ishvalans known?

But this time, a different question came first. “So.” Ed asked, “Who decides what happens to this?”

Miles looked at him. So did Scar.

“What?”

“Nothing,” Miles said. “There is a meeting today about exactly that.”

The meeting was louder than expected.

“It must not be touched,” an old man said. “It is in the old records too.”

A young man on the other side shot back at once. “If we’re only going to live by the old records, should we leave the ruined houses as they are too?”

“That is a different matter.”

“How is it different?”

“It is stone that was used in prayer.”

“If people starve because of a stone, is that what Ishvala wants?”

The murmuring grew louder. Opinions were split among the residents too. Full preservation. Partial mining. Use only as much as needed for reconstruction. No export. Sell it, but use the profits for Ishval’s reconstruction. Preserve only the areas around the places of prayer.

Ed sat by the wall, listening.

One man looked at him. “The Fullmetal Alchemist.”

“Former.”

“You’re the one who surveyed this stone, right?”

“I did.”

“Then tell us. What’s the right thing to do?”

The meeting room went a little quieter.

Ed opened his mouth. “The mineral itself doesn’t have any special power.”

“So it’s fine to dig it up?”

“That’s not for me to say.”

The man frowned. “You’re the one who surveyed it.”

“So I can tell you what the stone is,” Ed said. “But what to do with it is a different matter.”

“What’s the difference?”

Ed thought for a moment. “I’m not the one who’ll be affected by that stone.”

It went quiet.

Ed glanced at Scar. “I read the old records too. Your brother’s records too.”

“And?”

“I can tell you what people in the old days thought,” Ed said. “But people from the old days can’t decide for the people living now.”

“My brother did not try to go back to the past either,” Scar said.

“I know.” Ed looked at the residents. “So I can give you information.”

The properties of the mineral. The deposits. Its value. The old records.

“What to do after seeing all that is for the people who live here to decide.”

No conclusion came out of it that day. Instead, a few things were settled.

The mineral strata directly connected to places of prayer will not be touched for the time being.

Other areas will not be mined immediately either.

More residents’ representatives will be brought together.

The actual value of the mineral, the expected revenue, and the cost of reconstruction will be made public.

If outside purchase offers come in, their terms will also be made public.

Until a final decision, neither the central government nor the military will take mining rights.

It was decided by agreement of the reconstruction committee, and Miles agreed as the military’s representative and would report it to Central as it was. In three months, they would meet again in the same place to review the decision.

When the meeting ended, Ed asked, “That’s it?”

Miles gathered his papers. “For today.”

“You didn’t decide anything.”

“We did.”

“What?”

“To not decide yet.”

Ed closed his mouth.

“An answer you would like,” Scar said.

“Who says I like it?” Ed grumbled. But he was smiling a little.

Outside the meeting room sat a sample of the dark red mineral brought in during the survey. Ed picked it up.

At first, he had thought this stone was the answer. A place of prayer. The color red. Alchemy. The Truth. They had seemed like they would connect. They didn’t.

The stone was just a stone. But that didn’t mean it had no meaning. To the people of old Ishval, it was sacred. In Amestris, it was a material. In Xing, an ornament. For the people of Ishval now—it hadn’t been decided yet.

“That’s weird,” Ed said.

“What is?”

“It’s the same stone, but everyone sees it differently.”

“That is only natural.”

“Why do you always say the things I figure out the hard way are natural?”

“Because you are slow.”

“You want to get hit?”

Scar ignored him and walked on.

Ed put the mineral back where it had been. He didn’t take it.

As the sun went down, Ed went back to the room he used to stay in.

As he unpacked, a notebook fell to the floor. A tattered cover. Desert sand still caught in the gaps.

Ed sat on the edge of the bed and opened it. In the front pages were written, one after another, Ishvala, the stone, the Sage of the West, Karsa, Kairun. Some things were wrong, some were crossed out, and some were still blank.

And a little after that:

Who decides?

Ed’s hand stopped. Ishval. Karsa. Lansi. Haojin. Mulanhe. Kairun. Zhenha. Luhan. Ling.

People who wanted to open a road. People who wanted to close it. A man who tried to get rid of the emperor. A man who tried to become emperor. And a man who tried to shrink how much the emperor decided.

Ed picked up his pen.

Who decides?

Below it he wrote:

The people affected have to be part of the decision.

He looked at it for a long time. Something about it bothered him. It sounded too much like the right answer. Ed clicked his tongue. Below that, he wrote again.

At the very least, you can’t decide while leaving them out.

This time he didn’t cross it out.

He moved down to the next question.

Why do only Amestrian alchemists see the Gate?

Blank.

The next.

What is Ishvala?

Also blank.

Ed started to close the notebook, then stopped. He went back to the first page and turned through it again.

There was a lot he’d gotten wrong. Some things he had connected too quickly. Things that were similar, he’d assumed had the same origin. He had even made a wrong story out of things that were all true. He had thought that if good people decided, the results would be good. And because he had seen the Truth—he had thought he was a little closer to the answers.

Ed snorted. “Give me a break.”

He had seen the Truth. So what? Seeing what was beyond the Gate didn’t mean he had seen the whole world. If anything, the things he didn’t know had multiplied.

But strangely, it didn’t make him as irritated as it used to. There were still plenty of blank pages.

Ed looked at them and snorted. “Not bad.”

He’d come all this way looking for answers. And ended up with more questions.

Still—

it was a little fun.

Ed turned to the next page. He picked up his pen. At the very top, he wrote:

What I learned this time.

He looked at those words for a while. “This is going to be kind of long.”

Hammering sounded outside the window. Night was beginning in Ishval.

Ed started writing the first line.
