# Chapter 22: A Road Opens Both Ways

The next morning, as he finished his third plate, Ling said, "Today we head south."

"Why are you only telling me now?!" Ed shouted.

Xiaolin came through the door wearing a traveling coat. "I was going back to Haojin today anyway. I'll guide you."

"What about Chenluo?"

"He went ahead about the pastureland. We'll meet him in Mulanhe."

When Ling reached for a fourth dumpling, Ed pushed the plate away. "Stop eating and let's go!"

"My dumpling!"

* * *

About two hours south of Lansi, the road grew noticeably narrower. At first, two carts could pass side by side. A little farther on, only one. And once they turned onto the branch toward Haojin—

"You call this a road?" Ed looked down at his feet. Two faint lines of wheel ruts ran through the sand and gravel.

"It's worse when it rains," Xiaolin said, walking ahead.

"How much does it even rain in the desert?"

"That's why when it does, the road disappears."

"Then it's not a road!"

Ling laughed from horseback. "But we're still getting there."

"You're on a horse!"

Several sacks of dried medicinal herbs were tied to Xiaolin's pack.

"You take those all the way to Lansi every time?" Ed asked.

"Yes."

"By cart?"

"When a cart can get through."

"And when it can't?"

Xiaolin patted her shoulder. "People carry them."

Ed looked at the road again. When he had first heard the words "southern trade road," he had pictured something much grander. New caravans coming and going. Merchant houses springing up. A road that would compete with Lansi. But for the people here, maybe just having the road they already had be a little less broken would make a big difference.

* * *

Haojin was too small to call a city, but it was where goods from the surrounding villages gathered. Houses and shops lined the inside of a low earthen wall, and medicinal herbs were laid out in rows to dry on wide wooden platforms.

When Xiaolin walked in, people greeted her from all sides.

"Back again?"

"You came back quick this time."

"How are prices in Lansi?"

"Later." Xiaolin pointed behind her. "I've got guests today."

Everyone's eyes went to Ling.

"It's uncomfortable every time this happens," Ling muttered.

Ed snorted. "I thought you were born to be emperor."

"That's about mealtimes."

At the village entrance, a cart was being loaded. Two young men were tying ropes. Xiaolin went over to them.

"Where are you headed?"

"Lansi."

"Both of you?"

"No. Just him." One pointed at his friend beside him. "He says this time he's not coming back."

"Why?" Ling asked.

The one who was leaving shrugged. "There's not much work here."

"What about farming, or herding?"

"It's not my land, and they're not my animals."

"And in Lansi?"

"A relative works at a warehouse. He said they're hiring."

Ling thought for a moment. "If a new road opened in the south, would you stay?"

The young man looked at the cart. "I don't know."

"Why not?"

"A road opening doesn't mean I'd have work."

"If people come, work comes," Xiaolin said.

"Maybe." The young man pulled the rope tight. "Then maybe I'd come back."

"But for now, you're going?"

"For now, there isn't any."

Ling didn't ask anything more.

Ed looked at Xiaolin. A road opens, so the young people stay. It wasn't that simple. But there really were people leaving because there was no road.

* * *

In one corner of the Haojin market, herb trading was in full swing. Sacks of the same herbs Xiaolin had brought were piled up. A merchant from Lansi opened a sack, looked inside, and named a price.

Ed frowned. "That's it?"

The merchant looked up. "What's it?"

"That was way more expensive in Lansi."

Xiaolin sighed. "That's why I keep talking about a road."

"So take it there yourselves," the merchant said.

"And the transport costs?"

"Not my business."

"And if the cart breaks?"

"Also not my business."

"And people having to go along for days?"

The merchant shrugged. "That's why I come all the way out here to buy."

"How much do you sell it for in Lansi?" Ed asked.

"Why would I tell you that?"

"Roughly."

"Don't want to."

"Why you little—"

From behind, Ling grabbed Ed by the collar. "Calm down."

"Let go!"

"The Emperor will ask about the price."

The merchant's face stiffened. "...Your Majesty?"

Ed glared at Ling. "Isn't that cheating?"

In the end, the merchant admitted roughly what he sold it for. The gap between that and what he paid was large. But once you took out the cost of the cart, the guards, food, lodging, breakage, and losses, it wasn't all the merchant's profit.

Ed clicked his tongue. "The gap's still big."

"If we could move it ourselves, it would be different," Xiaolin said.

Ed looked down at the herbs. They were the same goods. But their price changed depending on where they were. And what lay in between was—

The road.

* * *

Farther south of Haojin, the houses thinned out. Wide pastureland appeared in their place. Flocks of goats and sheep could be seen in the distance.

Before the afternoon was over, they reached Mulanhe. It was smaller than Haojin. The houses were scattered far apart, and at its center was a large well ringed with old stones.

Chenluo was standing in front of it. "You're late."

"How could we be quick on a road like that?" Xiaolin said.

"Which is why you want to build a road, I suppose."

"If you know that, why ask?"

Ling stepped in between them. "Don't start fighting right off the bat today too."

Chenluo bowed his head. "I wasn't trying to fight."

"You two always start like that," Ed muttered.

Chenluo led Ling to the well. "This is the place I wanted to show you."

Ling looked down into it. "How much water does it give?"

"It changes with the seasons."

Ling thought for a moment. "No."

Chenluo looked at him.

Ling asked again. "Who uses it now?"

Ed turned to look at Ling. Chenluo didn't answer for a moment either. Then he counted them off. "The people of Mulanhe."

"And?"

"Two groups who use the western pastures."

"And?"

"There are herders who move with the seasons."

"Merchants?"

"They use it now and then, even now."

"And if caravans started passing through all the time?"

Chenluo looked down into the well. "That's the problem."

"Not enough water?"

"Not usually."

"But?"

"In the dry season, it's different," Chenluo said. "If you only write down the amount of water in a ledger, it might look like there's plenty to spare."

Ling nodded slowly. "Then what we need isn't a count of wells. We also need to know who uses them, and when."

For the first time, Chenluo seemed satisfied.

"That's still no reason to give up on the road," Xiaolin said.

"I didn't say we'd give up." Ling put his hand on the edge of the well. "I'm saying let's find out whether the water that *can* be used..." A pause. "...is the same as the water that *should* be used."

* * *

As it happened, a market was being held on the outskirts of Mulanhe. Herders had brought livestock and hides, and nearby villages had laid out dried food and medicinal herbs. It was small, but there were a lot of people.

Chenluo stopped walking. "Those people."

In one corner of the open ground, two unfamiliar merchants were talking with residents.

"Who are they?" Ed asked.

"People from the Lansi side."

"What are they doing all the way out here?"

"These days they go around asking about land."

Ed stopped. "Already?"

"They're not saying they'll buy yet," Chenluo said. "Whose land it is. How much it is. Whether anyone's thinking of selling."

Ling's expression hardened.

When they approached, the merchants stopped talking.

"What were you talking about?" Ling asked.

One of the merchants answered. "Just asking about land prices a little."

"Why?"

"In business, you have to look into things in advance."

"Because of talk that a southern road is coming?"

The merchant hesitated for a moment. "In Lansi, everyone's saying there's a chance."

Ed cut in at once. "Nobody's even said they're building a road yet!"

"But it could happen, couldn't it?"

"Who says?"

"How would I know?"

Ed opened his mouth, then shut it. He remembered the first conversation he'd had with Furong. He had asked if it was possible. Furong had said it might be possible. That was all. But before anyone knew it, the imperial court was considering it. There would be relay stations. They would use the wells. And now people had shown up asking about land prices.

"The construction of a southern trade road has never been approved," Ling said.

The merchants looked at him.

"I won't forbid you from looking into land."

"Then there's no problem, is there?"

"But if any actual buying starts, report it to the government office."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know too."

"Are you going to forbid it?"

Ling thought for a moment. "I haven't decided anything yet."

The merchants backed off. Even Xiaolin said nothing this time.

There was no road yet. But just the talk that a road might come—had already set people moving.

* * *

As the market was winding down, Ed sat in the shade beside the well and opened his notebook.

Ling stuck his head over. "What are you writing?"

"Don't look."

"You tried to look at my records like that."

"That's different."

"How is it different?"

"Because it's mine."

"By that logic, the imperial records are mine."

"Then is all of Xing yours too?"

Ling opened his mouth. Stopped. "...That's a little weird."

"Exactly."

Ed moved his pen.

*You have to think about where the information you find ends up going.*

He thought of Karsa. The people there had known what roads and knowledge could bring with them, and so they had erased the road. But was that the only answer?

"Done?"

"Yeah."

"Now show me."

"No."

"Stingy."

Just then, a voice came from behind them. "Does Your Majesty intend to protect the land of the An?"

They both turned at the same time. A young man they'd never seen before was standing there. Dust-stained clothes. Nothing about him particularly stood out.

"Who are you?" Ling asked.

The man didn't give his name. Instead he asked again, "Will you stop outsiders from buying land?"

"Nothing's been decided yet."

"Then why are you investigating?"

"Because I need to know before I decide."

The man's eyes went to the well for a moment. "To protect something," he said, "doesn't that mean someone could take it away?"

Ling's expression changed slightly. "It could."

The man looked at Ling a moment longer. Then he turned away.

Lan Fan took a step. "Your Majesty."

Ling raised a hand. "Leave it."

"He's suspicious."

Ed watched the man's back too. "I think so too."

"He only asked a question."

"He didn't even give his name."

Ling didn't answer. The man had already vanished among the people at the market.

* * *

Before they headed back to Lansi, Chenluo took them to the pastures west of Mulanhe.

"There was a dispute here once."

"What kind of dispute?" Ling asked.

"The rights to the pasture and the well changed all at once."

"Who changed them?"

Chenluo hesitated a moment. "An imperial decree."

Ling's steps slowed. "And then?"

"A few years later, they went back."

"Why?"

"The emperor changed."

Ed looked at Ling. Ling looked at Ed. It was the same story Ling had been reading in the archive for days. Rights changed by one emperor's order, and brought back by another emperor's order.

"What about the people who were hurt by it?" Ling asked.

"Their families are still around here."

"Can I meet them?"

"Today would be difficult."

"I'd like to meet them next time."

Chenluo nodded.

In the shade of a rock a little way off stood the man who had questioned Ling at the market.

Someone beside him said, "But in the end, you got it back."

The man didn't answer for a while. His gaze passed over the pasture and the well, and went to Ling.

"That's exactly why nothing's changed."

"What do you mean?"

"It was the emperor who took it." A pause. "And it was the emperor who gave it back."

* * *

That night, Ed wrote briefly in a blank space in his notebook.

*Who decides?*

The tip of his pen paused for a moment. Below it, he left one more line in small handwriting.

*Does that decision survive the next person?*
