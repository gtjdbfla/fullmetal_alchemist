# Chapter 16: The Price of Information

The next morning, Ed sat in the inn's dining room, tearing pieces off his bread as he studied his notebook.

*The Sage of the West = Van Hohenheim?*

Below it, the things he'd established yesterday filled the page. Medicine that had already existed in Xing. Flow within the body and the earth. Alchemy brought from the west. What Hohenheim had learned in Karsa.

And—

"If you put all that together..."

"Mr. Edward Elric?"

Ed looked up. A stranger stood before him. About forty, in a long Xingese coat, with two rings on his fingers. Yesterday's jeweler stood behind him.

Ed put down his bread. "Who are you?"

"My name is Furong."

The man smiled. "I connect merchants here in Lansi."

"A broker?"

"Something like that."

"What's that got to do with me?"

Furong pulled out a chair. "The stone."

Ed looked at the jeweler. The jeweler quietly averted his eyes.

"...That was fast."

Furong was an information broker who had spent more than a decade connecting caravans and jewelers in Lansi. He set a red gemstone in front of Ed.

"In Xing, we call it Redvein Stone. The better the color and grain, the higher the price."

"In Amestris, it's called Crimstone. A material used in weapons. Extremely rare. Only tiny amounts exist."

Furong raised an eyebrow. "Used in blades on one side, worn around the neck on the other. Different names, but valuable in both."

When Ed heard the price, his eyes widened. "It's just a pretty rock."

"Does Amestris use gold only in industry?"

"...No."

What troubled Ed wasn't Furong's box. It was the interest in his eyes when he wondered how much of the same stone lay in Ishval.

Furong came to the point. "I understand it was found in Ishval."

"During reconstruction."

"Were there mines before?"

"No."

"Any records of mining?"

"None I've seen."

"Then this is the first time?"

Ed thought for a moment. "In modern times, probably."

"In modern times?"

"There were similar stones in an old prayer hall."

Furong's eyes narrowed slightly. "As decoration?"

"I don't think so."

The lines in the prayer hall floor. The stones set at each intersection. Scar stopping him when he'd tried to remove one.

"I think they used them for religious purposes."

"So the ancient Ishvalans knew about Redvein Stone."

"All we know is that they knew about a similar stone," Ed corrected at once. "We don't know yet whether it was Redvein Stone, or whether they knew how much was underground."

Furong nodded. Then he asked, "How many places in Ishval has it appeared?"

"Several."

"Several?"

Ed opened his mouth, then stopped. He'd already spoken too freely once yesterday.

"I'm not saying any more."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know exactly either."

Furong smiled. "More cautious than yesterday."

"You weren't there yesterday."

"A pity."

"Not remotely."

* * *

Furong tried another question. "If it were transported from Ishval to Lansi, which route would it take?"

Ed frowned. "Transported?"

"Hypothetically."

"Nobody's even decided to sell it yet."

"I know."

"They might not even mine it."

"I know that too."

"Then why are you already thinking about transport?"

Furong watched him for a moment. "Because waiting until the decision to sell has been made would be too late to start looking for a road."

"..."

"A merchant has to move before the goods do."

Ed considered writing that in his notebook, then decided against it. Somehow, writing it down would feel like losing.

* * *

He had an appointment with Lien that morning. Ed went to the library.

Today, maps lay on the desk instead of old documents. Two of them. A current map and a much older trade map.

Lien pointed at the upper part of the current one. "This is the main route from Amestris today."

Northeastern Amestris. The northern desert. A series of oases. Then Lansi, in northwestern Xing.

"So not everywhere east of Amestris uses the same road."

"Of course not," Lien said. "Coming here from Ishval actually means a considerable detour."

Ed remembered his own route. Northeast from Karsa. Then north again. Joining the caravan road, then on to Lansi.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"Just thinking about walking it myself."

Lien tilted her head but didn't ask.

Ed pulled the older map closer. The south. A faint road leading from Ishval into the desert. In the middle—Karsa. And the road continued farther east.

"Where's this end today?"

Lien laid the maps over each other. "They don't line up exactly."

"Roughly."

"With the current boundaries..."

Her finger stopped far south of Lansi. "...near An clan territory."

"The An clan?"

"One of western Xing's clans."

"A big one?"

"Not small."

Lien pointed at the current map. "But they're away from the center of western trade now."

"Why?"

"There's no major road."

Ed looked down at the old map. There had been once.

* * *

"What about Lansi?"

"The Bai clan has considerable influence here."

"Does the clan own the city?"

"It's not that simple," Lien corrected immediately. "There are imperial officials, and a separate city administration. Plenty of people from other clans live here, too."

"Then?"

"But Bai clan members are heavily involved in landownership, caravans, and warehousing around Lansi."

"Because of the trade route?"

"Exactly."

Ed studied the current map. North. A thick road entering Lansi from northeastern Amestris. Then the old map. South. A faint road from Ishval through Karsa into western Xing.

They weren't the same road. They didn't converge. They ran along entirely different axes.

"...It's not a detour," Ed murmured.

"What isn't?"

"Nothing."

Ed pointed at the old map. "If this road existed again..."

Lien looked at him. "Could it?"

"Not the road through Karsa."

"Why?"

"I made a promise."

"To whom?"

"Can't tell you that either."

Lien sighed. Ed kept looking at the map.

"But it wouldn't have to be the exact same road."

Then he stopped. He remembered Furong's question that morning.

Which route would it take?

"...Oh."

* * *

That afternoon, Furong sat in an old caravan office in Lansi. Opposite him was a caravan master who had spent decades traveling between Amestris and Xing.

"Ishval?"

The man looked at the map. "You want to bring goods from there to Lansi?"

"Hypothetically."

"It's possible."

The man traced the current trade route. "Bring them up to the northern road."

"The cost?"

The man gave him a figure. Furong frowned.

"Expensive."

"Ishval's in the south."

"What about going straight across?"

The man laughed. "The desert?"

"Yes."

"Without a road?"

"If one were built."

The caravan master's laughter faded a little. "What would you be carrying?"

Furong took out the Redvein Stone. The man accepted it.

"Suppose there were a lot of this."

This time, he didn't laugh.

Furong laid the old map over the current one. The former trade corridor running east from Ishval headed farther south than today's Lansi. Its Xingese end lay close to An clan territory.

* * *

At sunset, Ed left the library and ran into Furong again.

Ed scowled. "Are you following me?"

"Lansi is my hometown," Furong said. "I'd rather ask you something."

"What?"

Furong unfolded a map. The current trade route in the north. A fresh pencil line in the south.

Ed's expression hardened. "What's this?"

"A hypothetical."

"What kind?"

"Suppose a new road were built from Ishval to western Xing."

The line didn't run directly through Karsa. Furong didn't know where it was. But it crossed much the same desert. And its eastern end was An territory.

"That way's dangerous," Ed said.

"Because it's a desert?"

"That too."

"Was there an old road?"

Ed closed his mouth. Furong watched him.

"So there was."

"There was."

"And now?"

"It's gone."

"Why?"

"Can't say."

Furong thought for a moment. "It doesn't matter."

"What?"

"Even without the old road..."

His finger followed the pencil line. "...we could build a new one."

"You think building a road is easy?"

"Not at all. It would need wells as well as a road."

Furong pointed at the Redvein Stone. "But if there's really a great deal of this, that changes things. It's still hypothetical."

Ed looked at the southern line on the map. A hypothesis was becoming a business calculation.

The current route was dependable. Wells. Villages. Guards. Warehouses. Infrastructure built by people over hundreds of years.

But starting from Ishval, it was a long way around.

The new southern route had nothing. That made it unusable now. But if it were built...

"...It's much shorter."

"Exactly."

"You'd build a road for Redvein Stone?"

"If it were profitable enough."

"For a gemstone?"

Furong smiled. "Once the road exists, we don't have to carry only gemstones."

Ed looked up. "What?"

"Amestrian metalware."

Furong counted on his fingers. "Xingese medicinal herbs. Textiles. Grain. Pottery. Livestock."

And then: "People."

Ed's expression changed.

"If the new road is fast and cheap enough..."

Furong pointed north, at Lansi. "...some of the goods coming this way now will go south instead."

"What happens to Lansi?"

"It could lose business."

"The Bai clan too?"

Furong's eyes shifted almost imperceptibly. "Where did you hear that name?"

"Today."

Ed looked at the map. "Then the An clan..."

"The opposite, presumably."

* * *

Only then did the two pieces of information connect. Ishval's stone. The vanished southern road.

At first, they had been entirely separate questions. A mineral found by chance during reconstruction. An old road discovered while following the traces of Scar's brother and Ed's father.

But to someone else, they were part of the same calculation.

"Who have you told about this?" Ed asked.

"I checked with a few people."

"How many?"

"About four."

"What if they tell other people?"

Furong answered as though it were obvious. "They will."

"Why?!"

"Because it's worth money."

Ed pressed a hand to his forehead. "Damn it."

"You didn't tell any lies," Furong said.

Ed looked up. "That's not the problem."

* * *

That night, Furong sat in a teahouse behind the market. Opposite him was a man who managed the ledgers of several Lansi caravans. He had close ties to the Bai clan.

Furong slid a sheet of paper across the table.

**Ishval, Amestris.**

**Discovery of a mineral believed to be Redvein Stone.**

**Exposed at multiple sites during reconstruction.**

**Deposits and quality unconfirmed.**

The man's eyes moved down.

**Current transport between Ishval and Lansi is inefficient.**

And the final line:

**If a direct southern route is established, An territory could become a new hub for western trade.**

The man looked up. "How much of this is certain?"

"We haven't even confirmed that it's Redvein Stone."

"Then the information isn't worth much, is it?"

Furong smiled. "Which is why it's cheap now."

"And once it's confirmed?"

"It'll be expensive."

"How expensive?"

Furong named a price. The man frowned.

"You're mad."

"By the time it's confirmed..."

Furong folded the map. "...the land will cost more than the information."

The man looked at the paper again. The An clan. And Lansi.

"May I show this to my superiors?"

Furong drank his tea. "It's your information now. You bought it."

* * *

At the same time, Ed sat at the desk in his inn room. He opened his notebook.

The left page.

*Hohenheim.*

Xerxes. Karsa. Xing.

The right page.

*Redvein Stone.*

Ishval. Used in an ancient prayer hall. Found in quantity during reconstruction. An expensive gemstone in Xing.

Below it, he sketched a simple map. North:

*Northeastern Amestris → Lansi.*

South:

*Ishval → ? → An territory.*

The two lines didn't meet.

Ed stared at them for a long time. Then he wrote:

*Was discovering the road the problem?*

A pause. He drew a line.

*No.*

Again:

*Was sharing the information the problem?*

He thought about that too. That wasn't it either. He didn't want to think that nothing would have happened if he had simply said nothing.

The stone existed in Ishval. That was a fact. It fetched a high price in Xing. That was a fact, too. Sooner or later, someone would have found out.

But—

Ed wrote a new sentence.

*I need to think about where the information I discover will go.*

He looked at it for a long time. That was as much of an answer as he could manage now.

It had been the same when he found the road in Karsa. He'd thought all he needed was to pass through. This time, too, he'd only asked questions. What the stone was. How much it cost. Where the old road had led.

Questions weighed nothing. A single sheet of paper could hold the answers.

But the people who heard those answers calculated prices. Calculated distances. And on a road that didn't even exist yet, they began calculating who would make money and who would lose it.
