# Chapter 7: The Good Fuhrer

Central. Mustang set aside the Ishval reconstruction report and the request for analysis of the rare mineral, and listened to a report on merchant movements.

"The analysis isn't even finished, and the mineral merchants are already moving in?"

"Rumors don't wait for results," Hawkeye said.

"We don't even know the deposit size, and they're this fast?"

"There's money involved."

That afternoon, the military, the resources ministry, the reconstruction officials, and civilian politicians would meet.

"Is Brandt coming?"

"Yes."

Mustang closed his eyes. "I already have a headache."

* * *

Erich Brandt was a civilian politician in his fifties. Gray hair. A well-pressed suit. And a man who made a point of opposing Mustang at least once, no matter what Mustang said. At least, that was how Mustang saw it.

He had worked in government agencies under Bradley's regime, too. That didn't mean he'd supported Bradley's military rule. Quite the opposite. He had criticized the very structure in which the military took over the nation's administration and politics. And he still did.

The Fuhrer had changed from Bradley to Mustang, nothing more. The fact that a soldier held supreme power over the state hadn't changed. That was his argument.

The meeting began. First, a military researcher spoke.

"The sample sent from Ishval closely resembles a previously confirmed rare mineral in outward characteristics."

Mustang asked, "You mean it's the same mineral?"

"Not yet."

"Before the detailed analysis?"

"Correct."

"Go on."

The researcher turned a page. "If it is the same mineral, adding it to certain alloys could raise their resistance to high temperature and pressure."

Someone asked, "Does it have military value?"

"There is a possibility."

"How much?"

"At this stage, it's difficult to say."

Mustang looked in his direction. "We don't even know what the stone is yet. Don't start thinking about weapons."

The room went quiet for a moment.

Next, an official from the resources ministry spoke. "What matters is the deposit size. Existing sources are all small. If a commercially viable deposit is confirmed in Ishval—"

"You're saying mining would be possible?"

"We could consider the possibility."

"Then a geological survey first."

"Yes."

Then Brandt spoke. "I don't think this should be viewed in terms of military value alone."

Mustang looked at him. "Then what?"

"Ishval's reconstruction needs a sustained source of funds."

Brandt was calm. "Right now it depends heavily on the central government's budget and military support. But we can't keep that up forever."

The reconstruction official nodded. Brandt continued. "If it is a commercially viable mineral, we aren't only talking about extraction."

He folded his fingers down one by one. "Roads."

"Transport."

"Refining."

"Related industries."

"Jobs."

"And, in the long term, tax revenue."

"Ishval might gain a foundation to sustain its own reconstruction."

Mustang thought for a moment. It wasn't wrong. If anything, it was a rather good story.

"So?"

"First, we must conduct a geological survey."

The resources official took it up at once. "We agree."

"Once the scale is confirmed, we can discuss whether to develop it."

Mustang asked, "Did anyone ask Ishval?"

The room went quiet. The resources official answered. "Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because we are currently at the survey stage."

"Doesn't a survey involve digging?"

"Small-scale drilling may be necessary."

"That's digging the ground, isn't it?"

The official's face stiffened. "Legally, separate consent is not required at this stage."

Brandt spoke next. "Under current law, major underground resources belong to the state."

Mustang's gaze turned on him. "Do you think that's right?"

"Strategic resources need some degree of central management."

"Interesting."

Brandt's eyebrow moved slightly. Mustang leaned back in his chair.

"Don't you dislike it every time I get involved in Ishval matters?"

"I opposed the Fuhrer deciding alone."

"You said that if I decided Ishval's future, it would be a dictatorship."

"I still think so."

Mustang tapped the papers with a finger. "But it's fine for the state to decide what's under Ishval's ground?"

Brandt fell silent. The people in the room glanced at each other.

"Because the law says so?"

"The law is not the same as an individual's judgment."

"Who wrote it?"

"The legislature and the government—"

"Is there any guarantee those people know Ishval's land better than the Ishvalans?"

Brandt didn't answer. The corner of Mustang's mouth rose.

"So you aren't perfect either."

Brandt looked at Mustang for several seconds. Then he said, "I concede that point."

The smile faded a little from Mustang's face.

Brandt went on. "That does not make leaving every decision to the Fuhrer the better answer."

"I never said that."

"But you could."

The room fell still.

"If you wished," Brandt said, "you could order a survey team dispatched, here, today."

"Or I could tell them not to go."

"Yes."

"So?"

"That is the problem."

Mustang's eyes narrowed. Brandt didn't look away.

"I don't believe you are the same kind of man as Bradley."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"I also think reform is necessary. Rebuilding Ishval is the right thing to do."

"You're being unusually pleasant today."

"But—"

Brandt said, "The claim that a good dictator isn't a dictatorship does not hold."

The air in the room hardened. Someone stared at Brandt. It was a dangerous thing to say to a Fuhrer's face.

Mustang didn't get angry. Instead, he tapped his chin with a finger. "So you oppose every decision I make?"

"No."

Brandt answered. "I am not someone who opposes all of the Fuhrer's decisions."

He paused.

"I oppose the fact that the Fuhrer can decide everything."

This time, Mustang didn't answer right away.

* * *

The meeting ran longer than expected. The conclusion was simple. A survey team would not be sent right away. First, they would consult with local residents on the scope of the survey. Religious sites, graveyards, and the like would be excluded. If the survey went ahead, local residents would take part in the process. And even if the deposit was confirmed, whether to develop it would be discussed separately.

The meeting ended. People filed out one by one. Brandt gathered his papers too.

Mustang called out. "Brandt."

"Yes?"

"About that decision just now."

Brandt turned. "I think it's a good decision."

Mustang laughed. "Looks like even a good Fuhrer comes along now and then."

Brandt didn't laugh. "That is what worries me."

"..."

Brandt bowed his head and left. The door closed.

Mustang looked at Hawkeye. "Can't I just leave that man out of the next meeting?"

"You could."

Mustang fell silent. Hawkeye went on tidying the papers as if nothing were wrong.

"That was a joke."

"I know."

"Then why did you answer that way?"

"Because it's true."

Mustang gazed at the door Brandt had gone through. "Everyone's being a pain to me today."

"Isn't that a good thing?"

"How so?"

"It means no one seems to be afraid of you."

"...That, you could be a little afraid of."

* * *

A few days later. Ishval. The people gathered at the town hall again. Miles read out Central's decision.

"The geological survey will proceed after consultation with the residents on its scope."

The crowd listened quietly.

"Religious sites and graveyards will be excluded. Ishvalan residents will take part in the survey process."

Someone asked, "So if we say no, they won't do it?"

Miles paused. "That, too, is a matter for consultation."

It wasn't a complete answer. The people noticed that, too. Still, the mood was better than last time.

Adin raised his hand. "When does it start?"

"It has not been decided."

"I'd like it to be soon, if possible."

An older man said, "Why the rush?"

Adin looked at him. "Because people are leaving."

"The land isn't going anywhere."

"People do."

The hall went quiet. Adin said, "If there's no work, young people leave."

"So you'd tear up any old place?"

"I never said that."

"Is money that important?"

Adin's face hardened. "Do we have to live poor because our parents died?"

He'd said the same thing at the last meeting. This time, no one rebutted him right away. From elsewhere, worries still rose.

"I don't want the military coming in."

"If a survey team comes, soldiers will come too."

"They'll say it's only a survey at first."

"And if they tell us to give up the land again?"

Voices overlapped.

Ed was listening from the back. Scar stood beside him. Ed asked quietly, "What about you?"

"About what?"

"The survey."

Scar thought for a moment. "I think it is acceptable."

Ed turned. "Really?"

"We should know what is there."

"And development?"

"That is a different matter."

Scar looked ahead. "Knowing what is there and digging it up are different things."

Ed nodded.

Then—

A low voice came from a little way off. "Did the Fuhrer give his permission?"

People turned around. It was Zahir, who had spoken at the last meeting. He was leaning against the wall again.

Miles said, "'Permission' is not the right word."

The man asked without smiling, "Then what should I call it?"

"Central told us to consult with the residents first."

"Central."

"Yes."

"The Fuhrer of the country that killed us."

The air in the hall changed. Adin looked at the man. "Mr. Zahir."

Zahir went on. "So this time he ordered them to ask us."

Miles's expression hardened. "Do you think that decision is wrong?"

"No."

It was an unexpected answer. Zahir said, "I think it's a good decision."

Adin asked, "Then what's the problem?"

"Who made it."

Zahir looked around at the people near him. "The ones who decide whether we may survey our own land are them."

A beat.

"The ones who decide we should wait are them."

And—

"The ones who decide they should ask us are them, too."

Zahir looked at Miles. "They are the ones."

Adin frowned. "Still, it's better than them doing whatever they want like before, isn't it?"

"It is."

"Then isn't that enough?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Zahir thought for a moment. "Because getting a good master doesn't make a slave a free man."

Faces stiffened here and there in the hall.

Miles asked, "Then what do you propose?"

Zahir didn't answer right away. "Nothing."

"...What?"

"Not yet."

Zahir pushed himself off the wall. "I only ask that we remember."

He walked toward the door as he spoke. "That a good order is still an order."

The door closed.

Ed stared in that direction for a while.

* * *

That evening, at Scar's house, Ed asked, "Mustang did the right thing, didn't he? He asked before the survey, and he left out the cemetery."

"Probably."

"Then Zahir's wrong?"

"Being Ishvalan does not mean I know what everyone thinks."

Ed fell silent. He took out his notebook and wrote *Is it enough if a good person decides?* After a moment, he added *And what if the next person is different?*

Bradley's face flashed through his mind.

"...I hate this."

Scar looked at him, but didn't ask anything more.

* * *

Central. Late at night. Mustang signed the last document.

*Preliminary Consultation Guidelines for the Ishval Geological Survey.*

Resident participation.

Religious sites excluded.

Separation of the development decision from the survey procedure.

Hawkeye took the document. "Shall I send it now?"

"Yes."

Hawkeye started toward the door and stopped. "Sir."

"What?"

"Do you think the next Fuhrer will make the same decision?"

Mustang looked up. It was an abrupt question. "What do you mean?"

"I was thinking about what Mr. Brandt said today."

Mustang put down his pen. "I'm different from Bradley."

"I know."

"I'm changing the system now."

"I know."

"The military, too."

"Yes."

"And Ishval."

"That's right."

Mustang leaned back. "It takes time."

"I know that, too."

Hawkeye said no more. Mustang looked down at his desk. Brandt's words came back to him.

> I oppose the fact that the Fuhrer can decide everything.

Mustang let out a short breath. "The next Fuhrer."

"Yes."

"That..."

Mustang thought for a moment. Then he said, "That'll be the next Fuhrer's problem."

Hawkeye said nothing. She took the document and left. The door closed. Mustang went back to work.

On one side of his desk lay the document he had just signed. At the very bottom: **Fuhrer's approval.** And beside it, *Roy Mustang.*

The decision he had made today was right. At least, he thought so. And the reason it had been possible was simple. It was because he was the Fuhrer.
