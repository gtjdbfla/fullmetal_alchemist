# Chapter 15: The City at the Western Edge

It was the fourth day since they'd left Karsa.

Ed stood in the middle of the road and flipped the map over. Turned it around again. Held it up to the sunlight.

"..."

Nothing changed.

A merchant leading a camel beside him asked, "Lost?"

"No."

"You're holding the map upside down."

Ed turned it right side up. "I was checking."

"Checking what?"

"...The direction."

The merchant didn't ask anything more. That felt worse.

* * *

After Karsa, the road didn't lead straight east. First northeast. Then north. After a few days of walking, small oasis villages began to appear.

It was just as Sahara had explained in Karsa. In the past, there had been a road leading east from Karsa. But now it was gone. Some stretches the people of Karsa had erased. Some stretches were buried in the desert. In some places, you couldn't even tell how far the road had once run.

So merchants today used a route farther north. An old caravan road across the desert. The current trade road linking northeastern Amestris and northwestern Xing.

On the second day, Ed had met a small caravan and joined it in exchange for some of his food and money.

"You're going all the way to Lansi?"

"That's right."

"Is it far?"

The merchant looked at Ed. "From where?"

Ed shut his mouth. He had decided not to tell anyone where Karsa was. "Just from here."

"About three days."

"Closer than I thought."

"There's a road."

Ed's expression changed for a moment. *There's a road.* In Karsa, that wasn't something anyone took for granted.

* * *

On the afternoon of the fifth day, a city appeared beyond the hill.

Ed stopped walking.

High city walls. Buildings of many stories rising behind them. Red roofs and blue tiles. Dozens of carts lined up in front of the gate. Camels. Horses. Wagons. People.

And above all, there were goods. Metalwork from the west. Xingese textiles. Medicinal herbs. Spices. Porcelain. He even saw wooden crates stamped with Amestrian lettering.

"This is Lansi?" Ed asked.

"It is," the merchant said. "One of the biggest trade cities in northwestern Xing."

Ed watched the carts going through the gate. "There's a lot of Amestrian stuff."

"Of course." The merchant pointed northwest. "Most of the big caravans from the west use that road."

Ed looked back. A road stretched on beyond the desert. It was different from the roads he had seen in Karsa. A road with no intention of hiding. A road made by countless people walking it for hundreds of years.

* * *

Getting through the gate alone took an hour. They had their luggage inspected. Wrote down their names. Said where they had come from. Wrote down the purpose of their stay.

The official looked at Ed's papers. "Amestris?"

"Yeah."

"Purpose."

"Research."

"What kind of research?"

"Old trade roads, and alkahestry, and my father, who might have come this way four hundred years ago."

The official looked up. "I'll put down sightseeing."

"Why?!"

"Next."

The people waiting behind him pushed Ed aside.

Lansi was different from Karsa. On both sides of streets that smelled of spices and medicine, shops went on without end. A city that had erased its roads, and a city that had grown big because roads gathered there.

Ed wrote in his notebook.

*Lansi, northwestern Xing. A major hub on the Amestrian trade road.*

He went from herbalists to doctors and finally reached the library.

"I'm looking for records from four hundred years ago about a sage who came from the west."

An old man looked up. "There'd be more of that in the capital."

"If he came from the west, there's got to be some trace here too. A man from Xerxes, with blond hair and golden eyes."

The old man looked at Ed's bangs. "Like you?"

"...Well, yeah."

"Wait a moment."

Lansi was an old city. Even before the current trade road was made, records and goods had gathered here from all over western Xing. But the roads of four hundred years ago were not the roads of today. Some roads had disappeared. Some villages had changed their names. In some places, the very direction the caravans traveled had changed.

The library held what was left of the doctors' records and caravan documents from that era. The problem was—

"I can't read this."

Ed glared at the book. It was ancient Xingese script. Some characters resembled the modern ones, but more than half were different.

"Of course you can't," the old man said.

"Isn't there a translation?"

"Some of it."

"Some?"

"Why would anyone have translated four hundred years of records?"

"Because there's a reason now!"

"A reason that showed up today."

Ed ground his teeth.

If Al were here, it would go faster. Al had already been in Xing for months, studying alkahestry with May. If Ed sent a letter—the time to reach the capital. The time for a reply to come back.

"...It'd be faster if I just went."

Even so, he needed to check whatever he could check here.

* * *

In the afternoon, the old man brought someone. A woman who looked about thirty, with her hair tied back and glasses on.

"This is Lien. She studies ancient documents."

Lien looked at the records Ed had spread out. "How far have you gotten?"

Of what he had copied in Karsa, Ed held out only the parts he could show. Xerxes. A practitioner from the west. A journey east.

Lien's hand stopped halfway down. "Karsa?"

Ed stopped too. "You know it?"

"Only the name."

"Do you know where it is?"

"No." Lien shook her head. "It's a place name that comes up now and then in old trade records. Nobody knows where it is now."

"Then that's fine."

"Where did you find this record?"

Ed opened his mouth, then closed it. "I can't tell you that."

"Why not?"

"I made a promise."

"To whom?"

"I can't tell you that either."

Lien looked at Ed for a while. "You have a lot of secrets."

"They're not my secrets."

At that, Lien didn't ask anything more.

* * *

An hour later, the first trace turned up. A doctor's record from four hundred years ago.

Lien read slowly. "'A strange man from the west.'"

Ed leaned in at once. "Keep going."

"'He did not understand our way of reading the pulse.'"

"And?"

"'But he showed great interest in the explanation of a flow within the body.'"

Ed's eyes lit up. "A name?"

"None."

The next line. "'He explained that the art of the west that he knew used the power of the earth to change matter.'"

Ed drew in a breath. It sounded like Amestrian alchemy. But this was from long before that. Most likely knowledge carried on from Xerxes.

"Next."

"'He asked why we speak of the flow of the body and the flow of the earth together.'"

"What did they answer?"

"It doesn't say."

"Why not?!"

"I didn't write it, did I?"

"...Fine."

Lien read the next line. "It says instead that a few days later he came back and brought a drawing of what he had understood."

Ed leaned in even further. "The drawing?"

Lien turned the page. There was nothing.

"...Why would anyone keep records like this?"

"I suppose they didn't know you'd show up four hundred years later."

* * *

The other records were much the same. In the east, there had already been medicine that read the flow of the body. There had also been a way of thinking that looked at the seasons and the changes of the land together. After the man from the west appeared, records about ways of changing matter using circles slowly began to show up. But the existing medicine and view of nature didn't disappear. If anything, different ways of describing things appeared side by side for a while.

"They mixed," Ed muttered.

Lien nodded. "That's how it looks."

"It's not that one person made all of it."

"From these records alone, at least, that'd be hard to say."

Ed opened his notebook.

*Hohenheim created alkahestry → X*

Below it, he wrote again.

*Existing Xingese medicine and view of nature + Western alchemical knowledge.*

He thought for a moment and added one more line.

*Includes knowledge picked up along the way, e.g. Karsa? → needs checking.*

Ed tapped the paper with the tip of his pen. His father hadn't come east carrying a finished answer. He had brought something from Xerxes. He had learned in Karsa. He had gone on east. There too he had asked questions. And he had left behind what he knew.

"...He really did keep wandering around."

"Who did?" Lien asked.

"My dad."

"Your father? That man from the west?"

"Yeah. Maybe."

"From four hundred years ago?"

"It's a long story."

"I'm curious."

"Really long."

* * *

It was nearly sunset by the time he left the library. Ed meant to go straight to his inn. But as he passed through the market, he stopped.

Deep red gems were laid out in front of a shop. Necklaces. Rings. Small ornaments. Each time the light hit them, the red veins inside seemed to overlap.

"...They look similar."

The shopkeeper reacted at once. "You have a good eye."

"No, that's not it."

Ed dug through his bag. The small sample Miles had given him when he left Ishval. The stone no one in Karsa had paid any attention to. He had brought it to check whether it was the same kind as the dark red stone he had seen in the prayer hall.

"Ever seen something like this?"

The merchant's expression changed. "One moment."

He took the stone. Held it up to the light. Took out a small lens. His face was different now.

"Where did you get this?"

"Ishval."

The merchant's hand stopped. "In Amestris?"

"Yeah."

"Things like this come out of there?"

"They found it during reconstruction work."

"How much?"

Ed thought for a moment. Before Karsa, he would have answered without a second thought. Still, what he had seen was true. The places where they'd dug for water pipes. Wells. Roads. Building foundations. And the red layer exposed at the construction sites on the outskirts.

"I don't know exactly."

"A little?"

"No," Ed said. "Quite a lot, actually."

The merchant looked at Ed. "...Quite a lot?"

"It's not even confirmed that this is the same as the stone you're thinking of."

The merchant held the stone up to the light again. "If it is the same, it's quite valuable."

"Why?"

"Because it's beautiful."

Ed paused. "...That's it?"

The merchant looked at him as if he were the strange one. "Does a gem need any other reason?"

* * *

Ed looked at the red gems in the shop again.

Amestrian military records said that a similar mineral called Crimstone went into special alloys for weapons. It was an expensive material, found in very few places and only in tiny amounts. It had the property of growing stronger under heat and pressure. That was why Ed had seen it as a material from the very start.

But in Xing, it hung on a necklace.

"How much is it?"

The merchant named a price. Ed's face stiffened.

"...For this tiny thing?"

"The color is good."

"It's a rock!"

"Gold comes from rock too."

"That's..." Ed started to argue, then gave up.

"Did they mine it in Ishval before?" the merchant asked.

"No. They found it during reconstruction. There were similar stones in an old prayer hall too."

"They knew it was there and never sold it?"

Ed thought of the prayer hall floor. Stones set wherever the lines met.

"I don't know why they didn't sell it. Maybe to those people it meant something different from a mineral or a gem."

The merchant tilted his head. Ed couldn't say for certain what the ancient Ishvalans had known either.

* * *

When Ed left the shop, he looked back once more. The red gems glittered under the lamps.

In the prayer hall in Ishval, a stone set into a dark floor. In Amestrian military records, Crimstone, a rare mineral used in weapons. In Xing, a costly gem.

A stone that might be the same thing, and people saw it in completely different ways.

"...Interesting."

And a moment later: "No." Ed remembered the price. "This could be kind of dangerous."

* * *

After Ed left, the merchant kept staring at the spot where he had looked at the sample.

The owner of the shop next door asked, "What was that?"

"A westerner brought in a strange stone."

"So?"

"It looked like Redvein Stone."

The neighbor's expression changed. "Really?"

"I'm not sure."

"Where did he say it came from?"

"Ishval."

"Something like that comes out of there?"

The merchant hesitated for a moment. Then he said, "He said there's quite a lot."

"How much?"

"He said he doesn't know himself."

"Then it's nothing."

"Maybe." The merchant looked toward the end of the market. "It's worth checking."

* * *

Back at the inn, Ed spread his materials out on the bed.

His thoughts went back to Hohenheim. Xerxes. Karsa. And the records left in western Xing. He still couldn't tell exactly which road his father had walked. He couldn't confirm whether he had passed through Lansi himself. But his knowledge from the west had reached this region. And he too had learned the knowledge of the east as he moved deeper in.

Ed wrote in his notebook.

*Sage of the West = Van Hohenheim?*

This time he didn't erase it. Beside it, he wrote:

*Very likely.*

And below that:

*But not the founder of alkahestry.*

*Existing Xingese knowledge + Western alchemy + knowledge from the many places he passed through.*

No single person had made it. No single country had made it either. People moved. Met. Asked. Learned. Handed over what they knew. And little by little, things changed.

His father had been one of those people too.

Ed lay down on the bed. "...I wonder how much Al's figured out."

The capital was still a long way off. Before that, there were things to check in Lansi. Traces Scar's brother had left in this region. The old western records. And the route Hohenheim had taken farther east.

Ed closed his eyes. He had found out a lot today.

And he hadn't yet thought about where the things he'd said would go.

* * *

That night, in a corner of the Lansi market, a merchant said to someone, "I heard an interesting story."

The story was still small. Southeastern Amestris. The reconstruction site in Ishval. A stone found there that resembled the red gems that sold for a high price in Xing. Whether it was really the same mineral, how much was buried there—nothing had been confirmed.

Only one phrase remained.

> "He said there's quite a lot."

Information was lighter than goods.

And so it always moved first.
