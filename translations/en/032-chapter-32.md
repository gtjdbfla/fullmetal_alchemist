# Chapter 32: The War That Started Again

Graus looked at Bark for a while.

Three reports lay on the desk. The first, a man. The second, a woman. The third, a child. The reports that had come in after that already outnumbered them. Military dead. Residents dead. Fighting at checkpoints. Roads closed. Wounded increasing.

Graus asked, "Why a child?"

Bark thought for a moment. "The first two were not enough."

"Not enough."

"Yes, sir."

"So you killed a child?"

"Yes, sir."

Graus's hand went still on the desk. "Why?"

"It was the most efficient way to carry out the mission."

Graus rose from his seat. "Efficient?"

Bark's expression didn't change. "After the first civilian death, the local command stabilized the situation. After the second as well."

"And?"

"A greater shock was necessary."

"And that was a child?"

"Yes, sir."

Graus said nothing for a while. Bark didn't make excuses. That made him angrier.

"I told you to avoid civilians."

"Yes, sir."

"And you killed three."

"You said to avoid them if possible."

Those words again. *If possible.* After the second one died, he could have said it plainly. He hadn't.

Graus looked straight at Bark. "From now on, do not kill civilians."

Bark asked, "Is that 'if possible'?"

"No." The answer came instantly. "Not 'if possible,'" Graus said. "Not a single one."

"..."

"There will be no targeting civilians on your own judgment." He took a step forward. "The same goes for your men."

"Understood."

"Do not interpret it this time."

"It is clear."

Graus let out a breath. "Go."

Bark saluted. Just as he put his hand on the door—

"Brigadier General!" An officer ran in. "Fighting again at the eastern checkpoint."

Graus turned. "Casualties?"

"One soldier confirmed dead so far. There are casualties on the residents' side too."

"Cause?"

"The two sides tell different stories. The soldiers say the residents fired first, and the residents say the army fired first."

Graus's gaze turned to Bark.

"It was not us," Bark said.

The officer confirmed it. "Second Lieutenant Bark's team was not in that sector."

Graus was silent for a moment. "Check it."

"Sir."

The officer went out. Bark moved toward the door again.

"Bark."

"Sir."

"The existing mission stands," Graus said.

"Understood."

"Keep monitoring the clashes and Central's reaction."

"Sir."

"But do not touch civilians."

"It is clear."

The door closed. Graus was left alone.

He had given the order not to kill civilians. The order to stop the war—he had not given. There was no reason to. Mustang hadn't even come yet.

* * *

Miles had hardly slept.

There were more marks on the map on his desk than the day before. The market. The western checkpoint. The southern road. The eastern residential district. At first they had been scattered points. Now they looked like a line.

"The west?"

"No major clashes overnight."

"The east."

"Fighting until dawn. It's quiet for now."

"The southern road?"

"We're blocking traffic."

Miles looked down at the map. "Fatalities?"

His adjutant gave a number. Miles's expression hardened. It had gone up.

He didn't know who had killed the first three people. He didn't know whether it had been the same person. But one thing about what was happening now, he could tell. It was no longer a situation moved by any one person's gun.

Among the people caught last night were ordinary residents. A man who had picked up a gun after hearing his younger brother was hurt. A man who had thrown stones after hearing a rumor that soldiers had searched his house.

The other side was no different. Soldiers who heard a comrade had been shot tightened the checkpoints. When the checkpoints got rougher, residents pushed back. The pushback made the soldiers tense again.

"The east is requesting reinforcements," his adjutant said.

"We will not send them."

"We're short on troops."

"And if we send more?"

"..."

"Over there, every soldier added means one more occupier."

"Then what do we do?"

Miles picked up his coat. "First we keep the ones already there from shooting."

"You're going yourself?"

"Yes."

"It's dangerous."

Miles opened the door. "That is why I am going."

* * *

Broken glass and stones were strewn in front of the eastern checkpoint. Soldiers stood on one side of the road. Residents stood on the other. On both sides, there were people with guns.

Miles walked between them. "Weapons down."

A soldier said, "Colonel, they have guns over there too."

"I know."

"Then why only us—"

Miles turned. "Are we at war right now?"

The soldier faltered. "...No, sir."

"Then do not act like it."

Miles looked toward the residents. "You too."

A man shouted, "It was the army that killed our people!"

"Did you see it?"

"Three people are dead!"

"Did you see a soldier fire?"

The man's face stiffened. "Then where's your proof it wasn't a soldier?"

"There is none," Miles said. "There is no proof that you did it either."

"So you're saying it was nobody?"

"I am saying we do not know." Miles looked back and forth between the two sides. "The moment you believe you know what you do not, you start shooting people."

After a long while, one resident lowered his gun first. One soldier followed. Others slowly moved. Not all of them. But no shots were fired.

Miles was satisfied with that. For now.

* * *

But elsewhere, it wasn't so.

Around noon, a military truck overturned. It was carrying food and medical supplies. Some said they were army supplies. Others said they were goods bound for the Ishval reconstruction zone. Before anyone could confirm it, people swarmed in.

"Our kid got shot yesterday!"

"A soldier of ours died!"

"Did we kill him?!"

"Then who did?!"

Someone shoved first. A man fell. A fist flew. Soldiers stepped in. A stone was thrown. One soldier grabbed the arm of another who was about to draw his gun.

There were no shots there. Instead, three people were carried off to the hospital.

In the afternoon, there were shots in another alley. A resident saw a soldier and hid. The soldier mistook it for preparation to attack. People nearby shouted that the soldier had raised his gun first. Even afterward, opinions were split on who had fired first.

Someone died. No one had planned it. But someone died.

* * *

Evening.

Graus set down the reports. "The eastern checkpoint."

"It was not us," Bark said.

"The southern road."

"No."

"The supply truck."

"No."

"The afternoon shooting."

"No."

Graus looked at Bark. "Your men?"

"They are moving as ordered."

"Civilians?"

"We have taken no direct action."

"Not one since I gave the order?"

"Yes, sir."

Graus closed his mouth. The order had been kept. Bark had killed no more civilians. And yet the situation hadn't improved. If anything, clashes were breaking out in more places.

Graus looked at the marks on the map. "So it continues even without us touching it."

"Yes, sir," Bark answered. "The conditions have already been created."

"Mutual hostility."

"Yes, sir."

Graus nodded slowly. The man he had been a few days ago might have called this a success. But it wasn't yet. The war had only just begun.

"Central?"

"They have requested further reports."

"Mustang?"

"He has not moved personally yet."

Graus's face hardened. "Then it is still not enough."

Bark didn't answer.

Graus looked at him. "Keep watching."

"Sir."

"The war is moving now without us laying a hand on it." Graus looked down at Ishval on the map. "What we need now is for Mustang to move."

* * *

Central.

Hawkeye spread out the reports one by one. Across from her, Mustang was reading the last page.

"That was fast," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Up to yesterday it was local unrest."

"Now there is fighting in multiple areas."

Mustang set the report down. "Miles?"

"He is prioritizing control of the local troops over additional reinforcements."

"He's doing well."

"I think so too."

Hawkeye took out the first report. "But there is something strange."

"What?"

"The beginning."

The first page. "An adult man. A civilian." The second. "The next day, an adult woman." The third. Hawkeye's hand paused for a moment. "And a child."

Mustang's expression hardened. "All three with the shooter unidentified?"

"Yes, sir."

"The same culprit?"

"No evidence."

"But?"

Hawkeye spread out the reports that came after. "From there on, the pattern changes completely."

Fighting at checkpoints. Scuffles between residents and soldiers. Attacks on military vehicles. Shootings in different places. The testimony was all over the place.

"The first three are similar," Hawkeye said. "One civilian dies, and the shooter is never seen."

"And after that?"

"People look at each other and fight."

Mustang looked at the first three pages again. "Who do you think lit the first match?"

"I do not know." Hawkeye answered at once. "Not yet."

Mustang looked at her.

"But it is worth confirming."

"Agreed." Mustang set the three pages aside. "Have all the records of the early incidents preserved. Take the witness statements again."

"Yes, sir."

"The troop deployments and reporting lines at the time too."

"Understood."

Mustang stood up. "Reinforcement requests?"

"They keep coming in."

"Hold off on any large-scale reinforcements."

"I believe that is better as well."

"There's no need to put in more troops and make it a real war."

Hawkeye looked at Mustang for a moment. "And if it is already a war?"

Mustang stopped moving.

A few seconds later, he picked up his coat. "Then I'd better see it for myself."

"You are going to Ishval?"

"Yes."

"If the Fuhrer moves in person, it takes on greater meaning."

"I know." Mustang folded the report. "But I think the time when I didn't need to go has passed."

* * *

That night, instructions from Central reached Graus.

**Preserve records related to the early civilian deaths.**

**Submit additional reports on the local chain of command and how the clashes arose.**

And last:

**The Fuhrer will assess the situation on site in person.**

Graus looked at that sentence for a long time. At last. Mustang was moving.

An officer asked, "Any further instructions, sir?"

"None."

"Should we increase the troops?"

"No."

"Then maintain the current posture?"

"Yes."

The officer saluted and went out. A moment later, Bark came in. "You called for me, sir."

Graus held out the paper. Bark read it.

"The Fuhrer is moving."

"Yes, sir." Bark put the paper down.

"What do you think will happen?" Graus asked.

"The Fuhrer will try to stabilize the situation in cooperation with the local command."

"Yes."

"He is likely to avoid a large-scale troop deployment."

"Yes."

"He will try to minimize civilian casualties."

Graus leaned back in his chair. It was exactly what he had predicted before. "And?"

Bark thought for a moment. "If the current clashes continue, it will be difficult to suppress them in a short time."

The corner of Graus's mouth moved slightly. "Yes."

"The second stage."

Graus looked at Bark. "Correct."

He had expected Mustang to come. From here on was what mattered.

"The existing mission stands."

"Yes, sir."

"There is no need for us to light any more fires." Graus looked at the marks on the map. "It is already burning."

Bark nodded.

"If the Fuhrer cannot suppress it," Graus said, "then I will end it."

Bark asked, "And if the Fuhrer does suppress it?"

Graus's gaze stopped. A moment later, he said, "He cannot."

"Are you certain?"

Graus didn't answer. He looked out the window.

In Ishval in 1908, he had been a lieutenant. Back then, orders were simple. Decide who the enemy is. Push. Finish it. Mustang had been there too. And now he was Fuhrer.

Graus said, "This time that man will try to do it differently."

"Yes, sir."

"Separating people."

"Yes, sir."

"Waiting."

"Yes, sir."

"Trying to kill even one fewer." Graus looked at Bark. "That is why he cannot end it."

Bark said nothing.

"Watch," Graus said. "Watch how Mustang ends it."

* * *

Graus took a telegram form out of the drawer.

*Commence movement. Destination: southern railway terminus. Pretext: joint mobile exercise.*

This time, he signed it.

* * *

That night, a military train pulled out of the southwestern garrison. The lights were out in every car. The soldiers had been told it was a joint mobile exercise.

"Nobody said anything about Ishval."

"They say the terminus is out that way."

"We're going all the way to the desert for an exercise?"

In the lead car, an officer checked his watch. "When we arrive, we wait for the next telegram."

He didn't say whose telegram.

* * *

At dawn the next day, word from Central reached Miles. The Fuhrer was coming.

His adjutant asked, "Will things get a little better now?"

Miles didn't answer. There were still many marks on the map. There had been two more clashes overnight. Fortunately, no one had died.

"People might calm down once the Fuhrer is here."

"They might."

"Or they might not?"

"One Fuhrer arriving does not make the bullets disappear."

Just then a messenger ran in. "Colonel. A report from the southern railway."

"Go on."

"A military train left the southwestern garrison last night. Right after His Excellency the Fuhrer left Central."

Miles looked up. "Where to?"

"It's coming up toward Ishval. It can reach the terminus in two days. The stated purpose is a joint mobile exercise."

"Who requested it?"

"We're checking."

Miles looked at the southern end of the map, at the railway terminus, for a long time. "An exercise."

"...Yes, sir."

"At a time like this."

He added one line to the report he had been about to fold.

*Southern railway: unidentified train.*

Miles folded the report. "Still." He picked up his coat. "One thing will get better."

"What's that?"

"Central will finally see it for themselves. That train included."

He went out. The eastern sky was slowly growing light.

* * *

Graus stood alone in front of the map. Mustang had moved. As planned. He believed he could control this war.

Graus took his hand off the map. Gunfire could still be heard in Ishval.

The war had started again.

Now only one thing remained.

**Who would end this war?**
