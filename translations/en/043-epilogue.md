# Epilogue: Eastward

The first caravan didn't come. A letter came instead.

It said that the first survey party for the planned section of the southern trade road had reached the An region. Two candidate sites for wells. One section that overlapped with pastureland. Three villages that hadn't agreed on terms of passage. A supply depot and campsite needed on the Ishval side. On the last page, the planned route was drawn.

Ed studied the map for a long time. "...What a mess."

"Because it's not a road yet," Miles said beside him.

There was already one thick, old line on the map. The road from the trade city in northeastern Amestris, past the desert oases, to Lansi. For a long time, it had been the main trade road traveled by caravans from Amestris and Xing. It had wells. It had campsites. It had warehouses. It had the foundation built by people and goods passing back and forth over a long time.

Compared to that, the newly drawn line was shabby. A road from Ishval across the desert into the An region. Where there were no wells, the line broke off. Where it overlapped pastureland, it went around. Whenever a village refused passage, it had to be drawn again.

The Ishval side wasn't any different. It was land that had been through war and oppression for a long time. There weren't enough warehouses to take in caravans. The roads weren't properly maintained either. For now, it wasn't a road that could compete with the existing trade road. It wasn't faster. It wasn't easier. It wasn't cheaper.

Ed looked down at the map. "It'll still happen, though."

"If it goes well."

"That's enough."

Miles looked at him.

Ed traced the thin new line with his finger. It didn't need to replace the existing road. It was just a second choice that hadn't existed before.

The same day, one more postcard came from Central. It was Hawkeye's handwriting.

> The last Fuhrer is buried in paperwork. He is eating, though.

Ed turned the postcard over. There was one more line on the back.

> Give my regards to Scar. — Hawkeye

* * *

In one corner of the map, there was a place where the line broke off. It was Karsa.

A road that had once connected Ishval with the east. A road people had once traveled, and so taxes and rights and rule had come along it. A road the people of Karsa had erased themselves.

The new route didn't head that way. It went far around.

"This part stays the same," Ed said.

"The Karsa side?"

"Yeah."

"I hear Xing left out any plan that passes through that area too."

"Good."

Miles looked at the detour on the map. "It will cost more because of it."

"I bet."

"Take longer too."

"That too."

Ed folded the map. "Still better than building a road on land where people said no."

Making a road wasn't the only answer. Not making one was a choice too.

* * *

The dark red mineral problem wasn't over either. The residents' meeting was already on its third session.

There was agreement on preserving the mineral strata connected to places of prayer. The problem was the rest. People who said sell some of it to pay for reconstruction. People who said don't let it leave Ishval. People who said even if it was mined, the residents had to manage it themselves. People who said more surveys were needed.

There was no conclusion.

Ed didn't stay until the end of the meetings anymore. If they asked about the properties of the mineral, he explained. If they asked about the old records, he showed them. If they asked what it was worth in Xing, he told them as much as he knew. After that, he stepped out.

At first some people thought it was strange. Why didn't the person who surveyed it tell them the answer?

Ed gave the same answer every time. "I'm not the one who lives here."

That day, too, he came outside after finishing his explanation.

"Brother!"

A familiar voice. Ed stopped right where he was. He slowly turned around.

At the end of the road stood a young man carrying a huge bag. Behind him there was luggage. One crate. Two. Three.

Ed's face twisted. "...What the heck is that?"

Al smiled brightly. "Material."

"*That's* all of it?"

"No. There's more coming behind."

"Are you crazy?!"

"You said you wanted to look into things."

"Looking into things and moving a library are two different things!"

Al came closer, smiling. It had been months. He'd gotten a little thinner. His hair had grown longer. His clothes were covered in dust. But he was still Al.

"I'm back."

Ed looked at his little brother for a moment. Then he held out his fist. Al bumped his own fist against it, smiling.

"Yeah," Ed said. "Welcome back."

* * *

That night, the room turned into a mess in no time. The books and manuscripts Al had brought took over the floor.

"This is a medical record from western Xing."

"Uh-huh."

"These are old observation records of the Dragon's Pulse."

"Uh-huh."

"This is from before what we now call alkahestry was properly separated from medicine."

"Uh-huh."

"And this is what May found for—"

"Wait." Ed raised his hand.

"What?"

"Have you eaten?"

Al stopped. "Oh."

"Seriously, you."

"You haven't eaten either."

"I'm always like this."

"You're bragging about that?"

"Watch how you talk to your older brother!"

Al looked down at Ed. "But I'm taller than you now."

Silence. Ed's eyebrow twitched.

"...No dinner for you."

"Why?!"

It had been a long time. Fighting over nothing, too.

* * *

"So," Al asked over a late dinner. "What did you find?"

"A lot. The Sage of the West was Dad."

Al's eyes went wide. "Then Dad created alkahestry?"

"No. They influenced each other. Nobody knows where it started."

"I can't believe you're saying that."

"Shut up."

Al thought for a moment. "The records I saw are similar. The older the records, the more medicine and qi and the flow of the earth are all mixed together with no line between them."

Ed grinned. "See? I was right."

"You said you couldn't find where it started."

"That's what I was right about!"

"That's not finding it."

"Shut up!"

* * *

After they finished eating, the two of them sat on the floor. This time, Ed's notebook lay between them. Al turned through it one page at a time.

~~*Ishvala = the Truth?*~~

The line drawn through it.

*No basis. Being similar doesn't make them the same.*

*The stone.*

*Karsa.*

*Hohenheim.*

*Alkahestry.*

And Kairun.

Al's hand stopped. "Who's this?"

"It's a long story. One transmutation circle, but the transmutation didn't stop there."

"Different from alkahestry?"

"It was different. Who made it, who he learned it from, nobody knows."

Al thought for a moment. "That's interesting."

Ed grinned. "Right?"

They both reached for the books piled beside them at the same time. Then Al stopped. "Brother."

"What?"

Al was looking at the back of the notebook.

*Why do only Amestrian alchemists see the Gate?*

Below it was small writing.

*Hypothesis.*

Al read slowly.

*Does Amestrian alchemy put the alchemist himself at the center of the exchange?*

*Does it become a structure where the more you try to gain, the more you stake yourself as the price?*

*Is the Gate at the end of that?*

Al's hand stopped on the last line. What he had lost in front of that Gate long ago came back to him. Ed didn't interrupt. He waited.

Al looked up. "This is what you think?"

"Yeah."

"Are you sure?"

"No," Ed said right away. "That's why I wrote 'hypothesis.'"

Al blinked.

Ed snorted. "I'm not getting fooled this time."

"By who?"

"By me."

Al laughed. Ed laughed along with him.

* * *

A few weeks later, the southern trade road survey party came back to Ishval.

They weren't a caravan. They hadn't gone to buy and sell goods. They had been a survey party, sent to check where a road could go. To look at wells. To talk with villages about terms of passage. To meet people in the An region again.

They had more dust on them than when they left. One wagon wheel had been replaced with a new one. One person had a bandage around his arm. But they had all come back.

The new map was even more complicated than before. A road bent because of a single well. In one section, it took a long detour to avoid pasture. Some villages allowed passage. Some villages refused. So the line bent again.

As the line bent, one warehouse ended up left off the map. It belonged to a merchant who had gone ahead and built it on the Ishval section, staking out a spot early.

"What about him?" Ed asked.

A member of the survey party picked his words for a moment. "We've agreed to help him find a spot on the next section. What he spent building it... stays spent."

The village that refused wasn't wrong. The man who built the warehouse wasn't wrong either.

Ed didn't ask anything more. There was nothing he could ask.

Al looked at the map. "This is way more inconvenient than the existing trade road."

"Obviously," Ed said. "That one's been traveled by people for years, for decades."

The road from the old trade city in northeastern Amestris to Lansi. A road made by caravans going back and forth again and again. This one was different. Ishval was only now being rebuilt. There weren't enough warehouses. Not enough campsites. Not enough foundation for crossing the desert.

"Then is there any point in building it?" Al asked.

Ed looked at the map for a moment. "There is."

"Why?"

"If that road's all there is..." Ed pointed at the existing trade route. "...then when something goes wrong on it, there's nowhere else to go." Then he pointed at the thin new line. "This isn't a road to get rid of that one."

"Then?"

"It's making one more."

Al looked down at the map.

"A choice," Ed said.

Al smiled a little. "It's weird hearing you say that."

"Why?!"

"The old you would've said you'd find the one best road."

Ed started to argue, then closed his mouth. "...Okay, that's true."

* * *

The old road was marked at the bottom of the map too. The road that used to pass close to Karsa.

"This way's shorter," Al said.

"That one's not opening."

"Why?"

"Karsa said no."

Al looked at the map again. The new route went far around to avoid that road.

"It's a big detour."

"Yeah."

"And they'll need to make more wells."

"Yeah."

"And it costs more."

"Probably."

"And still?"

Ed shrugged. "Still better than forcing a road onto someone else's land when they said no."

Xiaolin jumped down from a survey party wagon. "Ed! Did you see? For the first time, people in Haojin lined up over talk of the southern road!"

"Lined up?"

"To ask about prices! If it becomes a road, the herbs can go out, and people can come in—"

Xiaolin was laughing without even stopping for breath. It was hard to believe she was the same person who had sat trembling in Lansi, clutching a sack of herbs.

Furong jumped down from the last wagon of the survey party. "I've come to settle up."

"Money again?"

"This time it's money on the new road."

Furong opened a crate. Inside were samples of Haojin herbs and drawings of candidate warehouse sites on the Ishval side.

"You have to move before the road appears. I gave you the information for free, so now I'll earn it back through trade."

Ed looked at Furong for a long time. "...Guess I don't have to stop you this time."

"You can stop me if you like. In the end, contracts are made by people."

Just then, voices rose from the survey party. Chenluo was arguing with an Ishvalan representative over how much well water could be used.

"That's the survey party's standard!"

"We don't know how much water it'll take once the caravans increase!"

"So we decide that going forward—"

"If you settle the road first, what say do we get after that?!" Chenluo counted off on his fingers. "The An villages' share. The pasture. The existing wells. Those come first."

Al looked at Ed. "Should we stop them?"

Ed looked over too.

A moment later, someone spread a map on the ground. An An man crouched down. The Ishvalan representative sat down across from him. Someone else cut in. Fingers moved again. A line was erased. Drawn again.

Ed turned away. "It's fine."

"You sure?"

"If they start fighting, we'll go then."

The two of them walked toward the hill. Behind them, the voices were still loud. But no swords came out. There was no emperor's order. No Amestrian army drew the line. No one decided the answer alone.

The road wasn't finished yet. But little by little, it was becoming a road.

* * *

On the way to the hill, they passed a construction site next to the market. Someone shouted from on top of the roof.

"Get more rafters up! The school roof goes up before autumn!"

It was Adin. Young Ishvalans in mud-covered work clothes, carrying bricks, shouted back. Nothing had been decided about the mine yet. Even so, Adin didn't stop working.

"Whether the stone turns out to be worth money or not, the kids' school has to go up!"

Ed looked up. "Still loud as ever."

"Work goes faster when it's loud!"

On the highest rafter, a name was written in ink.

*Yunus.*

Next to the construction site, Zahir was sitting on the steps in front of the old place of prayer. He was looking back and forth between the market and the place of prayer.

"Thinking things over again?" Ed asked.

Zahir looked up. "A good decision is not always good for Ishval."

"What do you mean?"

"What people want and what must remain on this land are sometimes different." Zahir looked at the place of prayer again. "We must begin by telling them apart. There is no answer yet."

Ed didn't ask any further.

* * *

When they reached the top of the hill, they could see all of Ishval. Houses being rebuilt. The market, open again. The old place of prayer. And the desert beyond.

"Brother," Al said.

"Hm?"

"So."

"So what?"

"The thing you wanted to know at the start."

Ed turned.

"Ishvala," Al said.

The wind blew. Ed took the notebook out of his bag. The question that remained to the very end.

*What is Ishvala?*

Below it, the page was empty.

"Not the Truth?" Al asked.

"There was no basis for saying so."

"Then a word for nature?"

"Could be."

"A real god?"

"Could be."

"Something else?"

"Could be."

Al laughed. "So you didn't find it after all."

Ed looked down at the notebook. In the old days, he would have hated hearing that. Didn't find it. Got it wrong. Failed. Didn't finish.

But now it was a little different.

There wasn't just one road in Karsa either. There wasn't just one beginning to alkahestry. Hohenheim hadn't known every answer either. Scar's brother had come back with what he'd learned and left questions for the next person. The new trade road wasn't the one best road found either. They were just making one more road people could take.

Ed closed the notebook. "Yeah."

"You don't know?"

"I don't know."

"Then what are you going to do now?" Al asked.

Ed laughed. "What do you mean, what am I going to do?" He put the notebook in his bag. "I don't know, so I'll look into it."

Al laughed too. "Where do we start?"

"With what you brought, first."

"It's twelve crates."

Ed stopped. "What?"

"Twelve, counting the ones still on the way."

"Your letter only said 'enough to send in boxes'!"

Al ran first.

"Hey!!"

"You have to read them with me!"

"Who told you to bring twelve crates of that stuff?!"

"You said you were curious!"

"*Reasonably* curious!"

The two voices faded down the hill.

In the desert, there were old roads. There were roads made wide by people traveling them again and again. There were roads people had erased themselves. And there were thin lines that you could hardly call roads yet.

It wasn't the fastest road. It wasn't the easiest road. It wasn't a road someone else had decided on.

It was just one more place to go, beginning to take shape.

Edward Elric didn't know how far that road would lead. He didn't need to know.

If he didn't know—

he could go and see.

The road hadn't ended.

It was only leading on again,

eastward.

*The End.*
