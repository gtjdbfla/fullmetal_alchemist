# Chapter 29: How to Move a Fuhrer

**Ishval, Amestris.**

There was fire in Ishval.

Not a big one. A single warehouse on the outskirts of the reconstruction zone. The flames burned half the roof before they went out. No one died.

"Bring more water!"

At Miles's voice, the soldiers moved. The residents carried buckets too.

Just as the fire was nearly under control, the warehouse owner grabbed a soldier by the collar. "The army did this!"

"What?"

"You came and inspected this place yesterday!"

The soldier knocked his hand away. "Are you crazy? Why would we set a fire?!"

People crowded around. The soldiers moved in too.

Miles stepped between the two of them. "Both of you, hands down."

"Colonel, this man started it—"

"I heard." Miles looked at the warehouse owner. "Do you have proof the army did this?"

"A soldier came yesterday."

"That is not proof."

"Then who did it?!"

"I do not know," Miles answered. "Which means that for now, no one is the culprit."

A few residents looked dissatisfied.

Miles turned to the soldiers too. "And you will not treat these people like arsonists either."

"We never said anything like that."

"You are saying it with your eyes."

The soldier shut his mouth.

Miles looked at the burned-out warehouse. The fire was out. But people were still watching each other. That bothered him more.

* * *

The report went up to Graus that evening.

**Warehouse fire.**

**Fatalities: 0.**

**No clash between military and residents.**

Graus read the last sentence twice. "None," he murmured.

The officer standing beside him said, "Colonel Miles handled it on site."

"I see." Graus set the report down. "He is good."

It was praise. But his expression said otherwise.

Other reports lay on the desk too. A dispute at a checkpoint. Missing construction materials. Residents protesting military vehicle traffic. Rebel slogans written on a wall.

Taken one at a time, none of them amounted to much. But they were all pushing people in the same direction. The army suspected the residents. The residents suspected the army.

Graus tapped Ishval on the map with his finger. "This much will not move Central."

The officer didn't answer. Graus didn't explain any further either.

Not yet.

* * *

A few days later, Miles heard gunfire at a checkpoint.

One shot. Then two.

The soldiers raised their rifles.

"Weapons down!" Miles ran in.

"Colonel, they fired first from over there—"

"Was anyone hit?"

"No, sir."

"Then there is no reason to shoot yet. Lower them."

"But the gunshots—"

"I said lower them."

Among the residents on the other side, there were people holding guns too. Miles shouted in their direction. "You too, put them down!"

"The army fired first!"

"Did you see it?"

No answer.

"I am asking whether you saw it!"

A moment later, one man lowered his gun. Others followed. The soldiers slowly lowered their muzzles too.

Miles checked the bullet marks. The wall. The ground. No one had been hit.

It was strange. It looked less like someone had been trying to kill and more like—someone had been trying to make noise.

Miles looked back. "Find the shooter."

"Sir."

"Check everyone on the army side too."

"The army too?"

"Both sides."

He looked again at the bullet marks left on the wall. He didn't know who had fired. Only one thing was strange. Gunshots that hadn't hit anyone had made people aim at each other.

* * *

That night, Graus received a new report.

**Shots fired at checkpoint.**

**No casualties.**

**Situation stable.**

The last line was the problem again. *Situation stable.*

Graus closed the report. "Miles," he said quietly. "He blocks better than I expected."

The man standing across from him waited without a word. A young second lieutenant. Bark.

He spoke little. He remembered orders exactly. Above all, no emotion came between the result and the process.

Graus needed a man like that. The only troops he could command here were the twelve escorts of the investigation, and of them, Bark was the only one who looked useful.

"Bark."

"Sir."

"How do you read the situation so far?"

"The likelihood of a clash has increased."

"And?"

"The local command intervenes before the critical point every time."

Graus let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I see it the same way."

"At the current level, Central is unlikely to intervene directly."

Graus looked at Bark. "Yes."

He was precise.

"What I want is not a small disturbance."

Bark waited.

"It is a war," Graus said.

The room went quiet. Bark's expression didn't change.

"On what scale?"

Graus looked at him for a moment. He wasn't surprised. He didn't ask why. Graus liked that.

"Enough to move the Fuhrer."

"To make him intervene personally?"

"Yes."

Bark asked, "If the Fuhrer comes himself, won't that end the situation?"

Graus's finger stopped on the map. "Mustang will not be able to end it."

"You think he lacks the ability?"

"The opposite. He knows how to end it."

Bark still looked as if he didn't understand.

Graus looked at Ishval. "Because he is a man who has killed people here."

A short silence.

"Mustang took part in the Ishvalan War of Extermination."

"Yes. He will have seen then how to end a war quickly." Graus's voice dropped. "Seize the ones you suspect. Shoot the ones who resist. Push all the way to the places that might resist." He lifted his finger from the map. "It is fast."

Bark asked, "But you think he won't do that now."

"He cannot."

"Why?"

Graus smiled briefly. "Because he is the Mustang of today."

He said no more than that. Bark didn't ask either.

Graus leaned back in his chair. "The Mustang of today will try to separate civilians from armed men. He will not fire first. He will meet the residents' representatives. He will not send in troops carelessly. He will spend time trying to kill even one fewer person."

Bark thought for a moment. "Then the conflict may become protracted."

The corner of Graus's mouth rose very slightly. "Yes."

"Is that what you want?"

"Yes."

Bark turned his eyes to the map. "So the goal is not the Fuhrer's intervention itself."

Graus looked at Bark. For the first time, he looked satisfied. "Correct." He pressed a fingertip onto Ishval on the map. "Mustang has to come here."

"And then?"

"He has to try to stop it the way he believes is right." Graus's finger didn't move. "And people still keep dying."

"So the Fuhrer can't end the war," Bark said.

"Yes."

"And after that?"

Graus answered. "I end it."

* * *

Bark was silent for a moment. "Then questions will be raised about the Fuhrer's ability to command."

"Questions being raised is not enough," Graus said. "People have to see it."

"See what?"

"That being a good man," Graus said slowly, "and being able to protect a country are different things."

Bark nodded.

Graus got up from his seat. "Mustang may be a good Fuhrer." It didn't sound like praise. "He rebuilds Ishval. He listens to people. He tries to set the past right." Graus looked out the window. "But the Fuhrer's seat is not a place for being a good man."

Bark said nothing.

"It is a place for making decisions when they are needed." Graus turned around. "If that man cannot do it..." He paused briefly. "I will."

"How far will the war be expanded?" Bark asked.

"Within a controllable range."

"Fatalities?"

Graus's expression hardened. "Minimized."

"I need a concrete standard."

Graus looked at Bark. "No all-out war. No collapse of the whole city."

"Yes, sir."

"An actual clash breaks out between the army and the Ishvalan residents, and a judgment goes up to Central that the local command alone cannot resolve it. That is enough."

"Understood."

"Once Mustang moves in person, the first stage is over."

Bark asked at once, "The first stage?"

Graus nodded. "Yes. What comes after matters more."

* * *

A moment of silence passed.

Bark asked again, "How are local civilians to be classified?"

Graus's eyes went to him. "Civilians are civilians."

"And if they take part in a clash?"

Graus thought for a moment. "Avoid them if possible."

Bark repeated, "If possible."

"Yes."

It was a short answer. In that moment, Graus thought it was enough. He hadn't told him to kill civilians. He hadn't authorized indiscriminate attacks. In the field, there were always exceptions. That much discretion was necessary.

"I will give you five of the twelve escorts."

"Yes, sir."

"No large unit. It would leave traces."

"Understood."

"The goal is not a massacre."

"Yes, sir."

"It is to make a war."

Bark nodded. "Enough that the Fuhrer has to intervene personally."

"Yes."

"And so that the Fuhrer cannot resolve it."

Graus looked at Bark for a moment. "Exactly."

Bark saluted. "I will carry it out."

He headed for the door.

"Bark."

"Sir."

Graus started to say something more. *Do not touch civilians.* He could have just said that. But he thought he had already set the standard a moment ago. *Avoid them if possible.* That was enough.

"Never mind."

Bark went out. The door closed.

* * *

A few days later, at a small checkpoint in Ishval, a military vehicle was blocked by residents.

There were no gunshots this time. A soldier shoved a resident, and the resident grabbed the soldier's arm. By the time Miles arrived, both sides were already glaring at each other.

"All of you, lower them."

"Colonel, they—"

"Is anyone dead?"

"No, sir."

"Then lower them."

The muzzles went down. Miles turned to the residents. "Please clear the road. Who did what first will be looked into separately."

"You're always just looking into it!"

An hour later, the road was open. There was no clash.

As Miles turned to go, a young soldier said quietly behind him, "Does someone have to die before we're allowed to shoot, Colonel?"

Miles didn't stop walking. "We stop it now because once someone dies, it is too late."

He did answer. How many more times he would be able to say the same thing, he couldn't tell.

He had stopped it again.

* * *

That night, Bark reported.

"The local command has stabilized the situation again."

Graus spoke while looking out the window. "I know."

"Incidents at the current level are likely not enough for the Fuhrer to move personally."

"Yes."

"Shall I proceed to the next stage?"

Graus didn't answer right away. He looked at Ishval on the map.

The Ishvalan Civil War. What would the Fuhrer of that time have done?

The question didn't take long. Graus believed he knew the answer. And what the Mustang of today would do—he believed he knew that too.

"Proceed."

"Sir."

Bark turned.

"Bark," Graus said.

"Sir."

"Remember." His eyes stayed on the map. "The target is not Ishval."

Bark waited.

"It is the Fuhrer," Graus said. "Ishval is only the place for moving the Fuhrer."

Bark asked, "Do we continue even after the Fuhrer moves?"

Graus slowly raised his head. "That is when it really begins."

Bark gave a short salute. "Understood."

The door closed. Graus was left alone.

Mustang would come. And he would try to end the war without killing the people of Ishval. Graus believed that was impossible.

He took a telegram form out of the drawer.

It was not addressed to anywhere in Ishval. It was for the mobile battalion he had once put together himself. It was under another commander now, but they were soldiers who would still move at a single line bearing his name.

He wrote only one line.

*Stand by.*

He put off sending it until after Mustang moved.

If Mustang failed—he would end it himself.

That was the war Graus meant to make.

* * *

At the same hour, Bark sat on the bed in his quarters and emptied his rifle's magazine. He counted the bullets one by one. He set three apart and put them in the inner pocket of his uniform.

His expression didn't change.
